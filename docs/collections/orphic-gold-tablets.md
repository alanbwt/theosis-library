## Sources

- Photograph: Wikimedia Commons, 'Orphic Gold Tablet (Petelia - British Museum, London).jpg', Jononmac46 2014, CC BY-SA 3.0 (4000 x 3000; cropped to the tablet at full resolution, 1340 x 820). The British Museum collection site answers scripts with a Cloudflare check; not used.
- Greek: Cecil Smith's reading, printed in J. E. Harrison, Prolegomena to the Study of Greek Religion (1903), p. 574 n. 1, transcribed from the page image of archive.org `prolegomenatostu00harruoft` (the OCR of the Greek is unusable) and checked letter by letter. Harrison's chapter note cites Kaibel IGSI 641; the appendix heading prints 638.
- English: Gilbert Murray's translation in his Critical Appendix (Harrison 1903, pp. 660-661), checked on the page images. Harrison's preface names Murray as author of the appendix. Murray's translation covers lines 1-11; lines 12-14 are fragments and have no English.

## Mapping method

One small tablet carries the whole text, so all three sections point at the one photograph (by design). Checked on the image: the outline of the tablet, the painted number 3155, and part of line 3 ('ΕΔΟΝ ΕΜΠΕΛΑΣΕΙ', σχεδὸν ἐμπελάσειας). The rest of the letters are too small and crumpled on this photograph to be read line by line.

## Gaps (not built) and why

- Thurii tablets A1-A4 and the Thurii 'roll' (Naples, Museo Archeologico Nazionale): Murray gives text and translation of the three Compagno tablets (A1-A3) and Caecilia Secundina, but no photograph of the Thurii tablets was found on Wikimedia Commons, archive.org or other open sources reachable by script. Comparetti's Laminette orfiche (1910) is not on archive.org.
- Caecilia Secundina (Rome, British Museum): Greek and English in Murray's appendix, but no open photograph found (British Museum site blocked to scripts; none on Commons).
- Eleutherna (Crete, Athens National Museum): Greek and English in Murray's appendix; no photograph found.
- Hipponion (Vibo Valentia, found 1969), Pelinna and Entella: photographs of Hipponion exist on Commons (CC licences), but these tablets were found or published long after 1930, so no public-domain Greek edition or English translation exists. The Thessaly tablet in the Getty Villa (photo on Commons) has the same problem.
