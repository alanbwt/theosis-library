About

The Project

Theosis Library is a verified primary source database for ancient texts. The premise is simple: in an age when AI can generate plausible-sounding ancient writing in seconds, the only durable form of authority is the photograph. You can fake a translation. You cannot fake the parchment.

Every section in this library meets three non-negotiable criteria:

  1. A photograph of the original document. The tablet, stele, papyrus, scroll, wall or manuscript page that carries exactly that section's text, from the holding institution or a published photographic facsimile. Each section says where on the image its text is.
  2. The verbatim original-language text. From a named scholarly transcription or edition (for example the Electronic Babylonian Library, the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae, the Codex Sinaiticus Project, Perseus). No AI generation. No paraphrase. Gaps in the document stay visible as gaps.
  3. A verbatim English translation from a named published source. Public domain or openly licensed (the King James Version for the Bible; scholarly translations such as Budge, Griffith, Legge, Evelyn-White, Lamb or the eBL editors elsewhere). The translator is named on every page.

Nothing else gets in. If we cannot deliver all three for a chapter, that chapter is not in the library.

Current Holdings

2,363 entries (9,017 sections) in 12 collections. Every section passes the three-criteria rule.

Mesopotamia

  • Epic of Gilgamesh (Nineveh tablets) (19 entries). The Standard Babylonian epic on the clay tablets from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh. Electronic Babylonian Library transliteration; English: R. Campbell Thompson (1928).

Old Testament

  • Leningrad Codex (929 entries). The oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Old Testament, all 929 chapters on their pages. Unicode/XML Leningrad Codex (tanach.us); English: King James Version (1611).

Greece

  • Homer, Iliad (Venetus A) (24 entries). All 24 books of the Iliad on the Venetus A manuscript, line by line from the Homer Multitext project. Munro & Allen (Perseus); English: Samuel Butler (1898).

Dead Sea Scrolls & Second Temple

  • Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) (66 entries). The complete book of Isaiah from Qumran Cave 1, a thousand years older than the medieval codices, column by column. ETCBC Dead Sea Scrolls transcription of 1QIsa-a; English: King James Version (1611).
  • Josephus (Vatican manuscripts) (1 entry). Josephus on Jesus, on three Vatican manuscripts. Niese, Flavii Iosephi opera (1885-1890), via Perseus; English: William Whiston (1737).

New Testament

  • Codex Vaticanus (220 entries). The Vatican Library's 4th-century Greek Bible, New Testament chapters mapped verse by verse to its pages. Tischendorf 8th edition (1869); English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Sinaiticus (699 entries). The oldest complete New Testament, with much of the Greek Old Testament, the Epistle of Barnabas and the Shepherd of Hermas. Every chapter on its own folio from the Codex Sinaiticus Project. Codex Sinaiticus Project transcription; Tischendorf 8th ed. for comparison; English: King James Version (1611); Brenton; Lightfoot.
  • Codex Bezae (45 entries). The Greek-Latin Gospels and Acts at Cambridge, with its distinctive Western text. Tischendorf 8th edition (1869); English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Washingtonianus (88 entries). The Freer Gospels in the Smithsonian, including the Freer Logion in Mark 16. Tischendorf 8th edition (1869); English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Alexandrinus (228 entries). The British Library's 5th-century Greek Bible, New Testament chapters on the 1909 photographic facsimile. Tischendorf 8th edition (1869); English: King James Version (1611).

Later Traditions

  • Quran, Codex Parisino-petropolitanus (33 entries). One of the oldest Quran manuscripts, in early Hijazi script, suras mapped to folios. Tanzil Uthmani text; folio mapping Corpus Coranicum; English: Marmaduke Pickthall (1930).
  • Poetic Edda (Codex Regius) (11 entries). The mythological poems of the Codex Regius, from the creation of the world to Ragnarok. heimskringla.no Old Norse text; English: Benjamin Thorpe (1866).

Why This Matters

AI can generate plausible translations of any ancient text in seconds. What AI cannot generate is a photograph of a manuscript. The scans in this library are the evidence. The original-language texts are verifiable against those scans. The translations are checkable against the originals. Every claim links to a source you can see with your own eyes.

As AI becomes more capable, the need for a verification layer becomes greater, not less. This is that layer.
