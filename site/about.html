About

The Project

Theosis Library is a verified primary source database for ancient texts. The premise is simple: in an age when AI can generate plausible-sounding ancient writing in seconds, the only durable form of authority is the photograph. You can fake a translation. You cannot fake the parchment.

Every section in this library meets three non-negotiable criteria:

  1. A photograph of the original document. The tablet, stele, papyrus, scroll, wall or manuscript page that carries exactly that section's text, from the holding institution or a published photographic facsimile. Each section says where on the image its text is.
  2. The verbatim original-language text. From a named scholarly transcription or edition (for example the Electronic Babylonian Library, the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae, the Codex Sinaiticus Project, Perseus). No AI generation. No paraphrase. Gaps in the document stay visible as gaps.
  3. A verbatim English translation from a named published source. Public domain or openly licensed (the King James Version for the Bible; scholarly translations such as Budge, Griffith, Legge, Evelyn-White, Lamb or the eBL editors elsewhere). The translator is named on every page.

Nothing else gets in. If we cannot deliver all three for a chapter, that chapter is not in the library.

Current Holdings

4,040 entries (16,987 sections) in 94 collections. Every section passes the three-criteria rule.

What Roman historians of the early 2nd century wrote about Christ and the first Christians: Tacitus on Nero's punishment of the Christians and the execution of Christus under Pontius Pilate (Annals 15.44), and Suetonius on Claudius expelling the Jews over 'Chrestus' (Claudius 25.4) and on Nero's punishment of the Christians (Nero 16.2), each shown on the 11th-century manuscript page that carries it. Fisher 1906 (Tacitus) and Ihm 1908 (Suetonius), via Perseus; English: Church and Brodribb (1876); J. C. Rolfe (1914).

New Testament

  • P52 (John Rylands fragment) (1 entry). The oldest known New Testament manuscript: a 2nd-century papyrus scrap of John 18 in the John Rylands Library, Manchester, both sides. INTF NTVMR diplomatic transcription (CC BY 4.0); English: King James Version (1611).
  • P46 (Chester Beatty II + Michigan) (70 entries). The oldest collection of Paul's letters: the papyrus codex P46 (c. 200), split between Dublin and Ann Arbor, page by page. INTF NTVMR diplomatic transcription (CC BY 4.0); English: King James Version (1611).
  • P66 (Papyrus Bodmer II) (21 entries). The most complete early copy of the Gospel of John: the papyrus codex P66 (c. 200), page by page from the Bodmer Foundation. INTF NTVMR diplomatic transcription (CC BY 4.0); English: King James Version (1611).
  • P75 (Papyrus Hanna 1) (30 entries). The oldest copy of Luke and one of the oldest of John: the papyrus codex P75 (c. 175-225) in the Vatican Library, page by page. INTF NTVMR diplomatic transcription (CC BY 4.0); English: King James Version (1611).
  • P72 (Papyrus Bodmer VII-VIII) (9 entries). The oldest copies of 1 Peter, 2 Peter and Jude, page by page on a 3rd/4th-century papyrus codex in the Vatican Library and the Bodmer Foundation, including 2 Peter 1:4, "partakers of the divine nature". INTF NTVMR diplomatic transcription (CC BY 4.0); English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Vaticanus (220 entries). The Vatican Library's 4th-century Greek Bible, New Testament chapters mapped verse by verse to its pages. Codex Vaticanus transcription (NTVMR); Tischendorf 8th ed. for comparison; English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Sinaiticus (699 entries). The oldest complete New Testament, with much of the Greek Old Testament, the Epistle of Barnabas and the Shepherd of Hermas. Every chapter on its own folio from the Codex Sinaiticus Project. Codex Sinaiticus Project transcription; Tischendorf 8th ed. for comparison; English: King James Version (1611); Brenton; Lightfoot.
  • Codex Bezae (45 entries). The Greek-Latin Gospels and Acts at Cambridge, with its distinctive Western text. Codex Bezae transcription (NTVMR), Tischendorf where none is open; English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Washingtonianus (88 entries). The Freer Gospels in the Smithsonian, including the Freer Logion in Mark 16. Codex W transcription (NTVMR, IGNTP) for Mark and John; Tischendorf elsewhere; English: King James Version (1611).
  • Codex Alexandrinus (228 entries). The British Library's 5th-century Greek Bible, New Testament chapters on the 1909 photographic facsimile. Codex Alexandrinus transcription (NTVMR); Tischendorf 8th ed. for comparison; English: King James Version (1611).

Early Christian

  • 1 and 2 Clement (Codex Alexandrinus) (2 entries). 1 Clement, the letter of the Roman church to Corinth, and 2 Clement, an early sermon, on the last pages of Codex Alexandrinus, with the lost passages on Codex Hierosolymitanus 54. Kirsopp Lake, The Apostolic Fathers I (1912), via First1KGreek; English: J. B. Lightfoot (1891).
  • Didache (Codex Hierosolymitanus 54) (1 entry). The Didache, an early Christian church manual, on the ten pages of the only complete Greek manuscript, Codex Hierosolymitanus 54 (1056). Kirsopp Lake, The Apostolic Fathers I (1912), via First1KGreek; English: J. B. Lightfoot (1891).
  • Gospel of Thomas (Nag Hammadi Codex II) (20 entries). The Gospel of Thomas, 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, in the only complete copy: the Coptic text of Nag Hammadi Codex II (4th century), pages 32-51, each saying linked to the photograph of its page. Coptic SCRIPTORIUM edition by Paul Dilley (v6.2.0, CC BY 4.0); English: Mark M. Mattison (public domain).
  • Akhmim Codex (Gospel of Peter) (1 entry). The Gospel of Peter from the Akhmim codex, the only manuscript of the work, page by page in Lods's 1893 photographic plates with his line-by-line transcription and Robinson's English. A. Lods, diplomatic transcription and heliogravure facsimile (1893); English: J. Armitage Robinson (1896).

Later Traditions

  • Quran, Codex Parisino-petropolitanus (33 entries). One of the oldest Quran manuscripts, in early Hijazi script, suras mapped to folios. Tanzil Uthmani text; folio mapping Corpus Coranicum; English: Marmaduke Pickthall (1930).
  • Poetic Edda (Codex Regius) (11 entries). The mythological poems of the Codex Regius, from the creation of the world to Ragnarok. Normalized Old Norse text (Gudni Jonsson); pages mapped from Wimmer and Jonsson 1891; English: Benjamin Thorpe (1866).

Why This Matters

AI can generate plausible translations of any ancient text in seconds. What AI cannot generate is a photograph of a manuscript. The scans in this library are the evidence. The original-language texts are verifiable against those scans. The translations are checkable against the originals. Every claim links to a source you can see with your own eyes.

As AI becomes more capable, the need for a verification layer becomes greater, not less. This is that layer.
