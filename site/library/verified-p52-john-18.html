P52 (John Rylands fragment): John 18:31-33, 37-38

        
        
Papyrus P52, Egypt (c. 125-175 AD)

        
John Rylands Library, Manchester, Greek P 457 (P52): photographs from Manchester Digital Collections; Greek from the INTF NTVMR transcription; English from the King James Version · INTF NTVMR diplomatic transcription (CC BY 4.0)

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
P52 is a fragment of a papyrus leaf, 8.9 by 6.0 cm, from a codex of the Gospel of John. Bernard Grenfell bought it in Egypt in 1920 for the John Rylands Library, Manchester, and Colin H. Roberts identified it in 1935. It is usually dated to the 2nd century (the library's catalogue gives mid to late 2nd century; NTVMR gives 125-175), which makes it the oldest known manuscript of any part of the New Testament. Its provenance is Upper Egypt, possibly Oxyrhynchus.



One side (the recto, with the papyrus fibres running across) carries parts of John 18:31-33, where Jesus is brought before Pilate; the other side (the verso) carries parts of 18:37-38, Pilate's question "What is truth?". Seven lines survive on each side out of about eighteen. Because text runs on from one side to the other, the leaf was part of a book (a codex), not a roll.



Each section below shows one side of the fragment. The Greek is the diplomatic transcription made by the Institute for New Testament Textual Research (INTF, Muenster) for its New Testament Virtual Manuscript Room. Only a few letters of each line are preserved; the transcribers' restorations of the lost letters are in square brackets.

Line 1 οι ιουδαι at the left and ημ on the detached piece at the right (John 18:31); line 2 ουδενα ινα ο λ; line 3 πεν σημαιν; line 4 θνησκειν ι; line 5 ριον ο π; line 6 και ειπ; line 7 (bottom) αιω, from ιουδαιων at the end of 18:33. Notes

        
    
          
          
  1. 
            Greek: the NTVMR diplomatic transcription, line by line as on the papyrus. Square brackets enclose letters that are lost or illegible and were supplied by the transcribers; a dot under a letter marks it as uncertain; [...] marks a gap. Nomina sacra and numerals keep the scribe's overline. Spelling is the scribe's (no accents or breathings). Words broken at a line end stay broken as on the papyrus. Corrections are not shown: the text is the first hand.
          
    2. 
          
          
  2. 
            English: the King James Version (1611), whole verses, for every verse that has text on the page. A verse that begins on one page and ends on the next is given in full on both. The KJV translates a later form of the Greek text, so its wording does not always match the papyrus; material differences (such as verses the papyrus does not have) are noted in the section.
          
    3. 
          
          
  3. 
            Restorations: the length of the lost parts of the lines fits the standard text of John 18:31-33 and 37-38. Scholars have debated whether the repeated words εις τουτο in 18:37 fit the line length; the restoration shown is the NTVMR transcribers'.
