Introduction

        
In the middle of the Theaetetus, while examining Protagoras' claim that man is the measure of all things, Socrates breaks off to compare two kinds of people: the man trained in the law courts, quick and servile, and the philosopher, who does not know the way to the market place but whose mind ranges over the earth and the heavens. The digression ends with the sentence that later Platonists, Philo and the Greek Church Fathers took as a definition of the human goal: "we ought to try to escape from earth to the dwelling of the gods as quickly as we can; and to escape is to become like God, so far as this is possible" (phyge de homoiosis theoi kata to dynaton, 176a-b). Becoming like god is defined as becoming just and holy with wisdom.



The pages shown are from the Clarke Plato (Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS. E. D. Clarke 39), copied in Constantinople in 895 for Arethas of Patrae. It is the oldest surviving manuscript of this dialogue, cited by editors as B. The passage runs from fol. 96v line 20 to fol. 99r, six pages of 34 lines. Each section below is one Stephanus subsection; the page or pages listed for it were identified by reading the first and last lines of each page on the image. The Greek shown is Burnet's critical text, so the copyist's spelling and some words differ from it in places (for example, at 173e the manuscript has pheretai where Burnet prints petetai, and at 176a it has the word alethe that Burnet brackets).
