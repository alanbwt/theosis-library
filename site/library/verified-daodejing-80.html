Introduction

        
Chapter 80 of the Daodejing (Laozi), shown on the two oldest nearly complete copies of the work: the silk manuscripts Laozi A and Laozi B, found in 1973 in Tomb 3 at Mawangdui, Changsha (Hunan), a tomb sealed in 168 BCE. Laozi A is written in a script between seal and clerical forms and does not avoid the personal name of the Han founder Liu Bang (邦); it is commonly dated before 195 BCE. Laozi B is in clerical script, avoids 邦, and is commonly dated 194-180 BCE. Both put the De part (chapters 38-81) before the Dao part (chapters 1-37), and a few chapters stand in a different order from the received text.
Each section gives the silk transcription column by column, with the Jicheng column number before each column (for Laozi B, 上 and 下 are the upper and lower halves of a column, photographed on facing plates), and links to the plate that carries those columns. □ marks a lost character; ∠, ‧ and ﹦ are marks written on the silk (﹦ repeats the character before it). Below the transcription is the received text of the Wang Bi recension, which is the text James Legge translated in 1891; the silk wording differs from it, most visibly in 恆 for received 常 (the received text avoids the name of Emperor Wen, Liu Heng), in the frequent final particle 也, and in loan characters such as 胃 for 謂.

Manuscript

            
            
            
            
Where on the image
Laozi A (甲本), columns 64 to 66, on plate p. 97 of Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng, vol. 1 (column numbers are printed under each plate; the columns run from right to left). Checked on the image: column 64 reads ‧小邦寡民使十百人之器毌用使民 (the opening of this chapter, with the end of the preceding text where shown). In this manuscript the chapter follows chapter 66, not chapter 79. □ marks a character lost on the silk (14 in this section). Transcription and column numbers: Wikisource 老子 (帛書甲本), which follows the Jicheng column numbering. The English is Legge's translation of the received text, shown below the silk transcription; the silk wording differs from it in many places, so compare the two Chinese texts for the variants.

          

          

          

            
Classical Chinese · Mawangdui silk transcription

                    
64‧小邦寡民使十百人之器毌用使民重死而遠送有車周無所乘之有甲兵無所陳
65□□□□□□□用之甘亓食美亓服樂亓俗安亓居鄰邦相望雞狗之聲相聞民□
66□□□□□□

            
            

              Compare: Received text (Wang Bi recension), the text Legge translated
              
小國寡民，使有什伯之器而不用，使民重死而不遠徙。雖有舟輿，無所乘之；雖有甲兵，無所陳之。使人復結繩而用之，甘其食，美其服，安其居，樂其俗。鄰國相望，雞犬之聲相聞，民至老死，不相往來。

            

            
          


          

            
English · James Legge (1891)

            
1In a little state with a small population, I would so order it, that, though there were individuals with the abilities of ten or a hundred men, there should be no employment of them; I would make the people, while looking on death as a grievous thing, yet not remove elsewhere (to avoid it).
2Though they had boats and carriages, they should have no occasion to ride in them; though they had buff coats and sharp weapons, they should have no occasion to don or use them.
3I would make the people return to the use of knotted cords (instead of the written characters).
4They should think their (coarse) food sweet; their (plain) clothes beautiful; their (poor) dwellings places of rest; and their common (simple) ways sources of enjoyment.
5There should be a neighbouring state within sight, and the voices of the fowls and dogs should be heard all the way from it to us, but I would make the people to old age, even to death, not have any intercourse with it.
