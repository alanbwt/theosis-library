The Sumerian Flood Story (Eridu Genesis): Nippur tablet CBS 10673

        
        
Sumerian scribes (Nippur tablet, c. 1800-1600 BCE)

        
Penn Museum CBS 10673 photograph via CDLI (P265876) + ETCSL 1.7.4 transliteration and English translation (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0); columns after Poebel 1914 · ETCSL 1.7.4 (from CBS 10673)

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The Sumerian Flood story, often called the Eridu Genesis, tells how the gods An, Enlil, Enki and Ninhursaĝa made mankind and the animals, how kingship came down from heaven and the first cities (Eridu, Bad-tibira, Larak, Sippar and Shuruppak) were founded, and how the gods sent a flood to destroy mankind. Enki warns the pious king Ziusudra, who survives the flood in a great boat for seven days and seven nights, offers sacrifice to the sun god Utu, and is given 'life like a god' and settled in Dilmun. It is the Sumerian telling of the Flood story also known from the Babylonian epics of Atrahasis and Gilgamesh, and it invites comparison with Genesis 6-9.
The text survives almost entirely on one tablet from Nippur, CBS 10673 in the Penn Museum, Philadelphia, written in the Old Babylonian period (about 1800-1600 BCE). Arno Poebel found it among the museum's tablets in 1912 and published it in 1914. Only the lower third of the tablet survives: the bottom of the three columns on the obverse and the top of the three columns on the reverse. Between about 32 and 38 lines are lost in each gap, so the story is far from complete. A small joined fragment (CBS 10867) sits at the top of the obverse in the photograph.
Each section below is one paragraph of the ETCSL translation (segments A to E are the surviving blocks of text). The photograph is the face of the tablet that carries the section, and the transliteration is marked with the column.
Gaps. The lost parts of the columns (noted in the English) cannot be shown. Two further manuscripts of the story are not included: a fragment from Ur (published in 2018, after the ETCSL edition) and a tablet in the Schøyen Collection (MS 3026) that is not yet fully published.

E 3-11 More and more animals disembarked onto the earth. Zi-ud-sura the king prostrated himself before An and Enlil. An and Enlil treated Zi-ud-sura kindly ……, they granted him life like a god, they brought down to him eternal life. At that time, because of preserving the animals and the seed of mankind, they settled Zi-ud-sura the king in an overseas country, in the land Dilmun, where the sun rises.
