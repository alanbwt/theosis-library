Ketef Hinnom amulet KH2, Israel Museum: photograph via Wikimedia Commons + text, transliteration and English from English Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0) · Wikipedia, 'Ketef Hinnom scrolls' (text and transliteration of KH2)

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The Ketef Hinnom amulets are two tiny rolled strips of silver found in 1979 by Gabriel Barkay in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom, on the slope above the Hinnom valley in Jerusalem. They date from the late 7th or early 6th century BCE, before the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem, and were worn as amulets. Unrolled and read, both carry words of the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24-26, 'The LORD bless thee, and keep thee: The LORD make his face shine upon thee', which makes them the oldest surviving text that is also found in the Old Testament. They are in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem.
This entry gives the smaller amulet, KH2 (about 11 by 39 mm): its twelve preserved lines in Hebrew letters, a transliteration and an English rendering, all from Wikipedia. Lines 5-12 contain the blessing. The top of the strip is lost. The larger amulet, KH1, is not included here because no openly licensed original-language text of it was available.

Manuscript

            
            
            
            
            
            
Where on the image
The twelve preserved lines are on the one inscribed side of the unrolled silver strip, which the first photograph shows whole (checked on the image: the strip mounted on a plate, broken at the top, with fine scratched letters running across its width; the letters are very small and faint, the strip being about 11 mm wide, and the detached pieces at the bottom come from the lower end). Lines are numbered from the top of the preserved text as in Wikipedia. Square brackets are restorations, not visible on the silver. Where the text is on the photographs: the strip is photographed upright, so each line runs across its width (right to left) and the lines follow one another from the broken top downwards, over the large upper piece (the upper two thirds of the first photograph) and on to the detached pieces below it; which line ends on which piece cannot be told from the photograph. The second photograph is the large upper piece alone at the full resolution of the source photograph. The letters are very small, fine scratches on crumpled, cracked silver, and at this resolution they show only as groups of short straight strokes spread over the piece (near its top, in the middle and in its lower part); single letters and line divisions cannot be read on the photograph, so the line numbers follow the published reading, not a count made on the image. No sharper open photograph of the strip was found (Wikimedia Commons and archive.org, searched 2026-11-12).
