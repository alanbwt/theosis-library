Introduction

        
Stephanus page 120 of Plato's Critias (120a-120e): the kings' oath and judgment; the laws binding the ten kings.



The Critias is Plato's full account of Atlantis and of the Athens that fought it: the land and city of ancient Athens (109b-112e), then the island of Atlantis, Poseidon's kingdom beyond the Pillars of Heracles, its royal city, plain, army and laws (113b-120d), and the moral decline of its kings (120e-121c). Plato left the dialogue unfinished; it stops in mid-sentence as Zeus begins to speak, and the manuscript shown here stops at the same words.



The manuscript is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, grec 1807, known to editors as Plato A. It was copied in Constantinople around 850-875 by the scribe of the so-called philosophical collection, and is the oldest surviving medieval manuscript of the Timaeus and Critias (tetralogies VIII-IX: Clitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias, Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters). Each page has two columns of 44 lines. The Timaeus fills fol. 114r-144v and the Critias fol. 145r-151r.



On the manuscript this page of text runs over fol. 150v col. a to fol. 151r col. a. Each section below is linked to the folio side(s) that carry it. The mapping comes from reading the first words of every column on the images and finding them in Burnet's text: fol. 150v col. a begins ‹τῆς βασιλικῆς πόλεως, τῶν δὲ ἐννέα› (Crit. 119b); fol. 150v col. b begins ‹-γίζοιεν πάντα τοῦ ταύρου τὰ μέλη› (Crit. 120a); fol. 151r col. a begins ‹τὸ βασιλικὸν καταλύειν ἐπιχειρῇ γένος, κοινῇ› (Crit. 120c).



The Greek is John Burnet's critical text (Oxford, 1902), not a transcription of this manuscript; the scribe's spelling, accents and occasional readings differ from it. The English is R. G. Bury's Loeb translation (1929).

Manuscript

            
            
            
            
Where on the image
Crit. 120a stands in Paris gr. 1807, fol. 150v col. a to fol. 150v col. b. Checked on the image (column openings read on the BnF photographs and found in Burnet's text, quoted in Burnet's spelling): fol. 150v col. a begins ‹τῆς βασιλικῆς πόλεως, τῶν δὲ ἐννέα› (Crit. 119b); fol. 150v col. b begins ‹-γίζοιεν πάντα τοῦ ταύρου τὰ μέλη› (Crit. 120a).

          

          

          

            
Greek · Burnet

            
αὑτῶν νόμους θύσαντες καθαγίζοιεν πάντα τοῦ ταύρου τὰ μέλη, κρατῆρα κεράσαντες ὑπὲρ ἑκάστου θρόμβον ἐνέβαλλον αἵματος, τὸ δʼ ἄλλʼ εἰς τὸ πῦρ ἔφερον, περικαθήραντες τὴν στήλην· μετὰ δὲ τοῦτο χρυσαῖς φιάλαις ἐκ τοῦ κρατῆρος ἀρυτόμενοι, κατὰ τοῦ πυρὸς σπένδοντες ἐπώμνυσαν δικάσειν τε κατὰ τοὺς ἐν τῇ στήλῃ νόμους καὶ κολάσειν εἴ τίς τι πρότερον παραβεβηκὼς εἴη, τό τε αὖ μετὰ τοῦτο μηδὲν τῶν γραμμάτων ἑκόντες παραβήσεσθαι, μηδὲ ἄρξειν μηδὲ ἄρχοντι

            
          


          

            
English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
all the limbs of the bull, they mixed a bowl of wine and poured in on behalf of each one a gout of blood, and the rest they carried to the fire, when they had first purged the pillars round about. And after this they drew out from the bowl with golden ladles, and making libation over the fire swore to give judgement according to the laws upon the pillar and to punish whosoever had committed any previous transgression; and, moreover, that henceforth they would not transgress any of the writings willingly, nor govern nor submit to any governor's edict

πείσεσθαι πλὴν κατὰ τοὺς τοῦ πατρὸς ἐπιτάττοντι νόμους. ταῦτα ἐπευξάμενος ἕκαστος αὐτῶν αὑτῷ καὶ τῷ ἀφʼ αὑτοῦ γένει, πιὼν καὶ ἀναθεὶς τὴν φιάλην εἰς τὸ ἱερὸν τοῦ θεοῦ, περὶ τὸ δεῖπνον καὶ τἀναγκαῖα διατρίψας, ἐπειδὴ γίγνοιτο σκότος καὶ τὸ πῦρ ἐψυγμένον τὸ περὶ τὰ θύματα εἴη, πάντες οὕτως ἐνδύντες ὅτι καλλίστην κυανῆν στολήν, ἐπὶ τὰ τῶν ὁρκωμοσίων καύματα χαμαὶ καθίζοντες, νύκτωρ,

            
          


          

            
English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
save in accordance with their father's laws. And when each of them had made this invocation both for himself and for his seed after him, he drank of the cup and offered it up as a gift in the temple of the God; and after spending the interval in supping and necessary business, when darkness came on and the sacrificial fire had died down, all the princes robed themselves in most beautiful sable vestments, and sate on the ground beside the cinders of the sacramental victims throughout the night, extinguishing all the fire that was round about the sanctuary;

Greek · Burnet

English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
and there they gave and received judgement, if any of them accused any of committing any transgression. And when they had given judgement, they wrote the judgements, when it was light, upon a golden tablet, and dedicated them together with their robes as memorials. Greek · Burnet

Greek · Burnet
