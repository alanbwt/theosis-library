Introduction

        
The Code of Hammurabi is the longest and best preserved law collection of ancient Mesopotamia. Hammurabi, king of Babylon (c. 1792-1750 BCE), had it carved on basalt stelae late in his reign. The stele in the Louvre (Sb 8) is 2.25 m high. At the top Hammurabi stands before the seated sun god Shamash, god of justice; below, the text runs in horizontal bands, called columns, read from right to left with the signs set upright in the old monumental manner. The stele was carried off to Susa in Elam as war booty in the 12th century BCE and found there by the French mission of Jacques de Morgan in 1901-1902. Many of its laws touch matters also treated in the law of Moses in the Old Testament, such as false witness, theft, kidnapping, slaves, deposits, goring oxen and the rule of like for like (Exodus 21-23, Leviticus 24, Deuteronomy 19-25).
This part holds laws 200-249: bodily injury and its payments, physicians, veterinary surgeons, branders, builders, boatmen, and hired oxen. Most laws begin with šum-ma, 'if'.
Each section below gives the Akkadian lines of the stele as transliterated by CDLI, with column and line numbers, R. F. Harper's English of 1904, and the photograph of the column that carries those lines. On the obverse each column is shown twice: a colour crop of the band on the stone (the column is the band in the middle of the image) and the same column in the photographic montage of the whole inscription distributed by CDLI, where every column is turned upright and labelled at the top. Reverse columns are shown in the montage. A law that runs over from one column into the next lists both.

Notes

        
    
          
          
  1. 
            Akkadian: CDLI transliteration of the Louvre stele. Square brackets mark signs lost on the stone; # marks a damaged sign and ! a sign the editors correct, as in the source.
          
    2. 
          
          
  2. 
            Line labels such as 'v 26' give the stele column and line. The obverse has 16 legible columns and the reverse 28; seven further obverse columns were erased in antiquity.
          
    3. 
          
          
  3. 
            English: R. F. Harper (1904), from the Wikisource transcription corrected against the printed book where the two differ. Harper's words in parentheses are his own additions for sense.
          
    4. 
          
          
  4. 
            Harper numbers the reverse columns continuously after the 16 obverse columns, so his col. 41 is reverse column xxv.
          
    5. 
          
          
  5. 
            Harper divides laws 133 and 176 into 133 and 133A, 176 and 176A; CDLI keeps one law number for each, so both parts appear in one section. Laws 242 and 243 form one law in both.
