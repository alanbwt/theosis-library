Introduction

        
In book 3 of the Laws, Plato's last and longest dialogue (c. 350 BCE), an unnamed Athenian, the Cretan Clinias and the Spartan Megillus ask where political society began. The Athenian's answer is a history of the human race on a scale of tens of thousands of years: civilization has been destroyed many times by floods, plagues and other disasters, and after the last great flood only a few herdsmen survived on the mountain tops, without tools, metals or writing. He traces how they lived, how households under the rule of the eldest joined into villages and then into cities with lawgivers, and how the first city was built in the plain at Troy. The passage is the fullest statement in Plato of the cyclical destruction of civilization that the Egyptian priests describe to Solon in the Timaeus and Critias.



The manuscript is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, grec 1807 (Plato "A"), written at Constantinople in the third quarter of the 9th century, one of the two oldest and most authoritative manuscripts of the Laws. Book 3 begins on fol. 174r; the passage occupies fols. 174r-176v, two columns to the page. Each section is one Stephanus section and points at the page(s) that carry it; the column breaks were read on the images and are recorded in each note.



This entry covers Laws 3.680a-683a on fols. 175r-176v.

English · R. G. Bury (1926)

                    
680awhat possible need of laws the men of that time had, and who their lawgiver was. [Clin.] Excellent. [Ath.] Shall we suppose that those men had no need of lawgivers, and that in those days it was not as yet usual to have such a thing? For those born in that age of the world’s history did not as yet possess the art of writing, but lived by following custom and what is called patriarchal law. [Clin.] That is certainly probable. [Ath.] But this already amounts to a kind of government. [Clin.] What kind?
