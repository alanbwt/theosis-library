Introduction

        
The goddess Ishtar proposes marriage to Gilgamesh. He rejects her and lists the fates of her former lovers. She asks her father Anu for the Bull of Heaven, which ravages Uruk until Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill it.
The Standard Babylonian version of the epic, traditionally ascribed to the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni, was written on twelve tablets. Most surviving copies come from the libraries of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and from Babylon and other Babylonian cities (7th-2nd century BCE). This page gives Tablet VI in the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition by A. R. George (2022 and later revisions).
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/VI) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is A. R. George's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 182 of the 183 lines of the eBL edition (99%), from 7 tablets in the British Museum: K.3990 (Nineveh), K.231 (Nineveh), K.4579.A (Nineveh), Sm.2112 (Nineveh), K.5335 (Nineveh), K.15193 (Nineveh), K.14945 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 1 of the 183 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 144 (only on VAT.9667). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
5Gilgameš put on his crown.

6The lady Ištar looked covetously on the beauty of Gilgameš:

7‘Come, Gilgameš, you be the bridegroom!

8Grant me your fruits, I insist!

9You shall be my husband and I will be your wife!

10Let me harness for you a chariot of lapis lazuli and gold,

11whose wheels are gold and whose horns are amber.

12You shall have in harness “storm-lions”, huge mules.

13Come into our house with scents of cedar!

14When you come into our house,

15doorway and throne shall kiss your feet.

16Kings, courtiers and nobles shall be bowed down beneath you,

17Produce of mountain and lowland they shall bring you as tribute!

18Your nanny goats shall bear triplets and your ewes twins,

19your donkey foal under load shall outpace a mule.

20At the chariot your horse shall gallop majestically,

21at the yoke your ox shall acquire no rival.

22[Gilgameš] opened his mouth to speak,

23[saying] to the lady Ištar:

24‘[If indeed I were] to take you in marriage,

25[...] myself and my clothing,

26[whence will come] my food and my sustenance?

27[will you feed me] bread fit for a god?

28[will you pour me ale] fit for a king?

29[...] should I bind,

30[…] should I pile high?

31[Would…] wrap […] in a cloak?

32‘[Why] would I take you in marriage?

33[You, a frost] that congeals no ice,

34a slatted door [that does not] block breeze and draught,
