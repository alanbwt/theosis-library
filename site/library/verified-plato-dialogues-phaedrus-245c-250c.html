Introduction

        
In his second speech on love in the Phaedrus, Socrates argues that every soul is immortal, since what moves itself never ceases to move. He then describes the soul in an image: a charioteer with two winged horses. The souls of the gods drive easily to the outer rim of heaven and look on what lies beyond it, 'the colorless, formless, and intangible truly existing essence'; human souls follow as they can, lose their wings and fall into bodies. The mind of the philosopher alone regains its wings, 'for he is always, so far as he is able, in communion through memory with those things the communion with which causes God to be divine' (249c).



The pages shown are from the Clarke Plato (Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS. E. D. Clarke 39), copied in Constantinople in 895, the oldest manuscript of the Phaedrus (siglum B). The passage runs from fol. 232v line 2 to the top of fol. 235r, six pages.
