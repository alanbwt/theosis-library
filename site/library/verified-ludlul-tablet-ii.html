Introduction

        
The sufferer reflects that he has done his duty to the gods and still suffers; he describes in detail the diseases that afflict him and the failure of the diviners and exorcists to help.
Ludlul bel nemeqi ('I will praise the lord of wisdom'), the Poem of the Righteous Sufferer, is a monologue in five tablets by Shubshi-meshre-Shakkan, a nobleman who lost the favour of his king and his gods, was struck by illness, and was restored by Marduk. It was composed in the late second millennium BCE and copied widely in the first millennium. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/2/SB/II) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 120 of the 120 lines of the eBL edition (100%), from 5 tablets in the British Museum: K.2518 (Nineveh), K.3291 (Nineveh), K.8396 (Nineveh), K.6935 (Nineveh), K.3972 (Nineveh).

English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
1One whole year to the next, the predictable time passed by,

2As I turned round about me, it was more and more terrible,

3My ill luck was on the increase, I could find no good fortune.

4I called to my god, he did not show his face,

5I prayed to my goddess, she did not raise her head.

6The diviner did not get to the bottom of it with divination,

7With incense the dream interpreter did not clear up my case,

8I appealed for a dream spirit, but it did not inform me,

9The exorcist appeased no divine wrath with rites.

10What doings, strange everywhere!

11I looked behind me: persecution, harassment!

12Like one who had not made libations to his god,

13Nor invoked his goddess at a food offering,

14Who was not wont to prostrate, nor seen to bow down,

15From whose mouth prayer, supplication were wanting,

16Who skipped holy days, made light of festivals,

17Who was neglectful and disregarded their rites,

18Who had not taught his people reverence and worship,

19Who did not invoke his god, ate his food portion,

20Who snubbed his goddess, brought no flour offering,

21Like one gone out of his mind, who forgot his lord,

English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
22Who casually swore a solemn oath by his god: I, indeed, became like (such a one)!

23I, for my part, was mindful of prayer and supplication,

24Supplication to me was the natural recourse, sacrifice my rule.

25The day for reverencing the gods was a source of satisfaction to me,

26The goddess’s ceremony was my profit and return.

27Praying for the king, that was my joy,

28And his fanfare was as if for (my own) good omen.

29I instructed my land to observe the god’s rites,

30The goddess's name did I drill my people to esteem.

31I made my praises of the king like a god’s,

32And taught the populace reverence for the palace.

33I wish I knew that these things were always pleasing to a god!

34What is good to one’s self could be an offense to a god,

35What in one's own heart seems abominable could be good to one’s god!

36Whoever could learn the reasoning of a god, the innermost core of heaven?

37A (goddess’s) intentions, the unknowable depths of the earth, who could fathom them?

38Where might mere human beings have learned the ways of the gods?

39He who was alive yesterday died today,

40For a moment one is downcast, in an instant full of cheer,

41In a wink he sings in jubilation,

42In a heartbeat he moans like a professional mourner,
