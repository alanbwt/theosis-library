Introduction

        
Callimachus of Cyrene wrote his Hymn to Delos in Alexandria in the 3rd century BCE. Near its end he tells how every year all cities send tithes and choirs to Delos, among them the peoples beyond the north wind, a very long-lived race. Their offerings of corn-sheaves pass through the Pelasgians of Dodona, the Malian land and Euboea; they were first brought from the Arimaspians by Upis, Loxo and Hecaerge, daughters of Boreas, who never went home, and in whose honour the girls and boys of Delos still offer their hair.



The manuscripts of Callimachus' hymns are all of the 15th century or later. The one shown is Paris, BnF, grec 2763, copied about 1470-1500 by Niccolò Leonico Tomeo, with the hymns on fol. 61r-88v (images from the BnF's microfilm, each a two-page opening, cropped here to the page). Lines 275-299 run from the foot of fol. 79v over 80r to the top of 80v. The Ambrosian manuscript B 98 sup. was also checked but is published with a no-download restriction.

English · A. W. Mair (1921)

            
Wherefore from that day thou art famed as the most holy of islands, nurse of Apollo’s youth. On thee treads not Enyo nor Hades nor the horses of Ares; but every year tithes of first-fruits are sent to thee: to thee all cities lead up choirs, both those cities which have cast their lots toward the East and those toward the West and those in the South, and the peoples which have their homes above the Northern shore, a very long-lived race. These first bring thee cornstalks and holy sheaves of corn-ears, which the Pelasgians of Dodona, who couch upon the ground, servants of the caldron which is never silent—far first receive, as these offerings enter their country from afar. Next they come to the Holy town and mountains of the Malian land; and thence they sail across to the goodly Lelantian plain of the Abantes; and then not long is the voyage from Euboea, since thy havens are nigh thereto. The first to bring thee these offerings from the fair-haired Arimaspi were Upis and Loxo and happy Hecaerge, daughters of Boreas, and those who then were the best of the young men. And they returned not home again, but a happy fate was theirs, and they shall never be without their glory. Verily the girls of Delos, when the sweet-sounding marriage hymn affrights the maidens’ quarters, bring offerings of their maiden hair to the maidens, while the boys offer to the young men the first harvest of the down upon their cheeks.
