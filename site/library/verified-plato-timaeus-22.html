Introduction

        
Stephanus page 22 of Plato's Timaeus (22a-22e): the Egyptian priest: 'you Greeks are always children'; floods and conflagrations.



Timaeus 20d-25d is Critias' first account of Atlantis: the story Solon was told by a priest at Sais in Egypt about an Athens of 9,000 years before, which defeated an invading power from the island of Atlantis beyond the Pillars of Heracles, after which the island sank into the sea. Plato returns to it at length in the Critias.



The manuscript is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, grec 1807, known to editors as Plato A. It was copied in Constantinople around 850-875 by the scribe of the so-called philosophical collection, and is the oldest surviving medieval manuscript of the Timaeus and Critias (tetralogies VIII-IX: Clitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias, Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters). Each page has two columns of 44 lines. The Timaeus fills fol. 114r-144v and the Critias fol. 145r-151r.



On the manuscript this page of text runs over fol. 116r col. a to fol. 116v col. a. Each section below is linked to the folio side(s) that carry it. The mapping comes from reading the first words of every column on the images and finding them in Burnet's text: fol. 116r col. a begins ‹ποιητὴς εὐδοκιμώτερος ἐγένετο ἄν ποτε αὐτοῦ› (Tim. 21d); fol. 116r col. b begins ‹πειρᾶσθαι διαμνημονεύων τοὺς χρόνους ἀριθμεῖν· καί› (Tim. 22b); fol. 116v col. a begins ‹ὑπὸ τῶν ποταμῶν φέρονται· κατὰ δὲ› (Tim. 22e).



The Greek is John Burnet's critical text (Oxford, 1902), not a transcription of this manuscript; the scribe's spelling, accents and occasional readings differ from it. The English is R. G. Bury's Loeb translation (1929).

καὶ δὴ καὶ τὰ παλαιὰ ἀνερωτῶν ποτε τοὺς μάλιστα περὶ ταῦτα τῶν ἱερέων ἐμπείρους, σχεδὸν οὔτε αὑτὸν οὔτε ἄλλον Ἕλληνα οὐδένα οὐδὲν ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν εἰδότα περὶ τῶν τοιούτων ἀνευρεῖν. καί ποτε προαγαγεῖν βουληθεὶς αὐτοὺς περὶ τῶν ἀρχαίων εἰς λόγους, τῶν τῇδε τὰ ἀρχαιότατα λέγειν ἐπιχειρεῖν, περὶ Φορωνέως τε τοῦ πρώτου λεχθέντος καὶ Νιόβης, καὶ μετὰ τὸν κατακλυσμὸν αὖ περὶ Δευκαλίωνος

            
          


          

            
English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
as were most versed in ancient lore about their early history, he discovered that neither he himself nor any other Greek knew anything at all, one might say, about such matters. English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
and by recounting the number of years occupied by the events mentioned he tried to calculate the periods of time. Whereupon one of the priests, a prodigiously old man, said, “O Solon, Solon, you Greeks are always children: there is not such a thing as an old Greek.” And on hearing this he asked, “What mean you by this saying?” And the priest replied, “You are young in soul, every one of you. For therein you possess not a single belief that is ancient and derived from old tradition, nor yet one science that is hoary with age.
