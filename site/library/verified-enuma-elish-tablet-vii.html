Introduction

        
The gods continue to proclaim the names of Marduk with their meanings. An epilogue asks that the names be remembered, taught and pondered by later generations.
Enuma Elish ('When on high'), the Babylonian Poem of Creation, tells how Marduk, god of Babylon, defeated Tiamat, created the ordered world and became king of the gods. It was written on seven tablets and recited in Babylon during the New Year festival. The date of composition is debated (proposals range from the 18th to the 12th century BCE); the surviving copies date from the 9th to the 2nd century BCE and come from Ashur, Nineveh, Kish, Babylon, Sippar, Uruk and other sites. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/2/SB/VII) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so (BM.37379, BM.37981, BM.39798). The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.

British Museum BM.34384: reverse, the tablet's own lines 22'b to 26'. eBL's corpus edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/2/SB/VII) attributes SB VII 158-164 to this manuscript at these column and line numbers. Photograph checked: BM.34384 alone, a narrow fragment with ink numbers 34384 and Sp. 503 and about eight worn lines; the joined pieces BM.35506 + BM.99642 (both faces densely inscribed) and BM.36146 are photographed separately and are linked as well. eBL treats BM.34384 + BM.35506 + BM.36146 as one manuscript and holds a separate photograph of each of these pieces; it does not say which piece carries which line, so the photographs of BM.34384 + BM.35506 + BM.36146 are linked, and the lines are on one or more of them. On this tablet lines 158, 161, 163 are entirely broken, of line 164 only illegible traces (x) survive and lines 159-160, 162 are partly broken (square brackets and x in the Akkadian). The English of these lines translates eBL's composite text, so it includes words that are not preserved on this tablet but are restored from other manuscripts or by the editors. Square brackets in the English mark eBL's restorations in its composite text; words that survive on other tablets carry no brackets even where this tablet is broken.

English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
158He wrote down and preserved for the future to hear,

159The destiny of Marduk whom the Igigi-gods exalted,

160Wherever water is the drink, his name let them invoke.

161Let them sound abroad the song of Marduk,

162How he defeated Tiamat and took kingship.

163[… An]u, Enlil and Ea, Belet-ili […]

164[… i]n? Babylon [and] Esagila […].
