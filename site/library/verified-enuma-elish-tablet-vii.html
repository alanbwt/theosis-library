Introduction

        
The gods continue to proclaim the names of Marduk with their meanings. An epilogue asks that the names be remembered, taught and pondered by later generations.
Enuma Elish ('When on high'), the Babylonian Poem of Creation, tells how Marduk, god of Babylon, defeated Tiamat, created the ordered world and became king of the gods. It was written on seven tablets and recited in Babylon during the New Year festival. The date of composition is debated (proposals range from the 18th to the 12th century BCE); the surviving copies date from the 9th to the 2nd century BCE and come from Ashur, Nineveh, Kish, Babylon, Sippar, Uruk and other sites. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/2/SB/VII) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 160 of the 166 lines of the eBL edition (96%), from 16 tablets in the British Museum: Sm.11 (Nineveh), K.2854 (Nineveh), Rm.366 (Nineveh), K.8522 (Nineveh), K.9267 (Nineveh), Rm.395 (Nineveh), K.17095 (Nineveh), BM.37379, BM.39798, BM.38706, K.13761 (Nineveh), K.8519 (Nineveh), K.7658 (Nineveh), K.4657 (Nineveh), K.18576 (Nineveh), BM.34384.
Lines not shown. 6 of the 166 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 50-52 (only on Ist-A.154, SU-1951.63); 54 (only on Ist-A.154, SU-1951.63); 78 (only on Ist-A.154, SU-1951.62); 106 (only on Ist-A.154, SU-1951.63, SU-1951.62). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.
