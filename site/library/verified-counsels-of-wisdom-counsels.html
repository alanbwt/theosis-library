Introduction

        
The advice warns against evil companions, careless and slanderous speech, quarrels and marrying a prostitute, and urges kindness to the needy, daily worship of one's god, and honest service.
The Counsels of Wisdom is a collection of Babylonian moral advice in about 160 lines, addressed by a father, perhaps a royal official, to his son: on speech, companions, kindness, worship and conduct. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/3/SB/-) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so (BM.51070, CBS.6059). The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 159 of the 159 lines of the eBL edition (100%), from 14 tablets in the British Museum and Penn Museum: K.13770 (Nineveh), BM.41931, BM.51070, K.8282 (Nineveh), K.3364 (Nineveh), BM.41682, CBS.6059 (Nippur), BM.33851, K.7897 (Nineveh), K.8231 (Nineveh), 1882,0522.555 (Nineveh), F.144, BM.33496, K.10652 (Nineveh).
