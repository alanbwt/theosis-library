Introduction

        
A hymn to Marduk, whose anger is fearful and whose mercy is great. The speaker tells how his gods abandoned him, omens turned against him, the king turned away, and courtiers plotted against him.
Ludlul bel nemeqi ('I will praise the lord of wisdom'), the Poem of the Righteous Sufferer, is a monologue in five tablets by Shubshi-meshre-Shakkan, a nobleman who lost the favour of his king and his gods, was struck by illness, and was restored by Marduk. It was composed in the late second millennium BCE and copied widely in the first millennium. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/2/SB/I) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 117 of the 120 lines of the eBL edition (98%), from 8 tablets in the British Museum: K.9392 (Nineveh), BM.36320, K.10503 (Nineveh), K.3291 (Nineveh), K.9237 (Nineveh), 1880,0617.2642, BM.38790, BM.32208.
Lines not shown. 3 of the 120 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 38 (only on SU-1951.10, IM.67628, BM.73592, BM.68444, IM.132669); 85 (only on VAT.11100, SU-1951.10, IM.67628, BM.73592, IM.132669, VAT.10756); 92 (only on VAT.11100, SU-1951.10, IM.67628, BM.73592, IM.132669, BM.61433). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.

English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
13In the brunt of his anger, tombs are readied,

14At the cast of his glance, he raises the fallen from annihilation.

15He glowers, protective spirits take flight,

16He regards, a man's god who has forsaken him comes back to him.

17His severe punishment is harsh and speedy,

18He draws up and, just in time, becomes like a mother.

19He is impetuous, charging forward like a wild bull,

20Like a cow with a calf, he keeps turning around watchfully.

21His scourge is barbed and punctures the body,

22His bandages are soothing, they heal the doomed.

23He pronounces, finding one guilty of many misdeeds,

24On the day of his justice, liability and transgression are absolved.

25He it is who afflicts with hellish tremors,

26By his sacral spell, chills and shivering are cast out,

27Who intensifies the … of Adad, the blow of Erra,

28Who reconciles the wrathful god and goddess.

29Sage lord, he divines the gods’ inmost thoughts,

30None among the gods can comprehend his ways.

31Sage Marduk divines the gods’ inmost thoughts,

32No god whatever can understand his mind.

33As heavy his hand, so compassionate his heart,

34As brutal his weapons, so life-sustaining his feelings.

35Without his consent, who could lighten his blow?

36Against his will, whoever could stay his hand?

37I, who touched bottom like a fish, can testify to his anger,

49Terrifying signs beset me,

50I was forced from my house, I wandered outside.

51My omens were confused, they were contradictory every day,

52With diviner and dream interpreter my way forward was unresolved.

53What I overheard in the street portended ill for me,

54When I lay down at night, what I dreamt was terrifying.

61So likewise the third “I will usurp his office!”

62“I’ll take over his household!” vows the fourth

63As the fifth subverts the speech of fifty,

64Sixth and seventh follow his evil example!

65The clique of seven joined forces against me,
