Introduction

        
Gilgamesh meets the ale-wife Shiduri by the sea, then the boatman Ur-shanabi, who takes him across the Waters of Death. Uta-napishti speaks to him about the fate of mortals.
The Standard Babylonian version of the epic, traditionally ascribed to the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni, was written on twelve tablets. Most surviving copies come from the libraries of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and from Babylon and other Babylonian cities (7th-2nd century BCE). This page gives Tablet X in the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition by A. R. George (2022 and later revisions).
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/X) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is A. R. George's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 276 of the 325 lines of the eBL edition (85%), from 3 tablets in the British Museum: K.8589 (Nineveh), BM.34160, K.3382 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 49 of the 325 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 47-60 (no manuscript preserves it); 126-130 (no manuscript preserves it); 196-220 (only on IM.67564); 257 (only on Rm.751); 292'-295 (only on Rm.751). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
1Šiduri was an ale-wife who lived by the sea-shore.

2there she lived, [in an inn by the sea-shore.]

3Potstands she had, and [vats of gold,]

4she was swathed in hoods and [veiled with] veils.

5Gilgameš came wandering, and […:]

6he was clothed in a pelt, and fearful [to look on.]

7He had the flesh of the gods in [his body,]

8but there was sorrow in [his heart.]

9His face was like one who had travelled a distant road;

10the ale-wife was watching him in the distance.

11Talking to herself she spoke a word,

12taking counsel in her own mind:

13‘For sure this man is a slayer of wild bulls;

14whence did he make straight for my gate?’

15The ale-wife saw him and barred her gate,

16barred her gate and went up on the roof.

17But he, Gilgameš, had (good) hearing … […,]

18he lifted his chin and turned [towards her.]

19Gilgameš [spoke] to her, [to the ale]-wife:

20‘Ale-wife, why when you saw [me did you bar] your [gate?]

21You barred your gate [and went up on the] roof.

22I shall strike the door, I shall [break the bolt.]

61[Then I was afraid …] … [… ,]

62[I grew fearful of death and so roam the wild.]

63The case of my friend was too [much] for me to bear,

64[so on a distant road I roam the] wild.

74What is its landmark? Give it to me!

77if it may not be done, I will roam the wild!’

78The ale-wife spoke to him, to Gilgameš:

79‘There never was, O Gilgameš, a way across,

80and since the days of old none who can cross the ocean.

81The one who crosses the ocean is the hero Šamaš:

82apart from Šamaš, who is there can cross the ocean?

83The crossing is perilous, its way full of hazard,

84and in between are the Waters of Death, that lie across the passage forward.

85So besides, Gilgameš, (once) you have crossed the ocean,

86when you reach the Waters of Death, what will you do?

87O Gilgameš, there is Ur-šanabi, the boatman of Ūta-napišti,

88and the Stone Ones are with him as he strips a pine in the midst of the forest.

89Go then, and let him see your face!

90if it may be done, cross with him,

91if it may not be done, turn around (and go) back!'

92When Gilgameš heard this,

93he took up (his) axe in his hand,

94he drew forth the dirk [from] his [belt,]

95he crept up and rushed down on them.

96Like an arrow he fell among them,

97(his) shout booming through the midst of the forest.

98Ur-šanabi saw him and clad himself in his aura,

99he took up an axe, all atremble before him.

100But he, Gilgameš, struck his head [and grabbed his hair],

101he pinned his arm along his chest.

102And the Stone Ones had sealed the boat,

103who did not fear the Waters of Death.

104Gilgameš strode into the Ocean, the wide sea,

105in the water he halted the boat and the Stone Ones,

106He smashed the Stone Ones, he tipped them into the river,

107He tied up the boat with a rope,

108and then [… he sat] down on the bank.

109[Gilgameš spoke to him,] to the boatman Ur-šanabi:

110‘So, Ur-šanabi, you took up an axe, all atremble before me!

111The fight and the battle you sought I will not wage against you.’

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
296[Enkidu indeed] they took to his destiny.

297‘[But you,] you toiled away, and what did you achieve?

298You are exhausting [yourself with] ceaseless toil,

299you are filling your sinews with pain,

300bringing nearer the end of your life.

301Man is one whose progeny is snapped off like a reed in the canebrake:

302the comely young man, the pretty young woman,

303all [too soon in] their very [prime] death abducts (them).

304No one sees death,

305no one sees the face [of death,]

306no one [hears] the voice of death:

307(yet) savage death is the one who hacks man down.

308At some time we build a household,

309at some time we start a family,

310at some time the brothers divide,

311at some time feuds arise in the land.

312At some time the river rose (and) brought the flood,

313the mayfly floating on the river.

314Its countenance was gazing on the face of the sun,

315then all of a sudden nothing was there!

316The abducted and the dead, how alike they are!

317They cannot draw the picture of death.

318The dead do not greet man in the land.

319The Anunnaki, the great gods, were in assembly,

320Mammītum, who creates destiny, made a decree with them:

321death and life they did establish,

322the day of death they did not reveal.’
