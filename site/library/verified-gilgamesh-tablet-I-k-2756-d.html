Introduction

        
Tablet I of the Epic of Gilgamesh (Standard Babylonian version, attributed to Sin-leqi-unninni) as it survives on one physical object: British Museum tablet K.2756.D (K.2756D + K.20778 (joined)), excavated at Nineveh from the library of Ashurbanipal (7th c. BCE). It is manuscript F₁ in A. R. George's 2003 edition. The photograph is the British Museum's, showing the inscribed face(s) and edges; column(s) iv, v are preserved (44 lines).
Three criteria are met for every section:
  1. Manuscript scan: the British Museum photograph of K.2756.D itself (served by the Electronic Babylonian Library, LMU Munich). Each section names the column and face (obverse = columns i-iii, reverse = columns iv-vi) and the tablet's own line numbers, so the reader can find the lines on the photograph.
  2. Original text: the eBL manuscript-level ATF transliteration of the signs on this tablet (not the composite text). Brackets [ ] mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign. Superscript numbers are the modern line numbers of George 2003 / eBL.
  3. Public-domain English: R. Campbell Thompson, The Epic of Gilgamish (London, 1928), verbatim, with his marginal line numbers as T n.
On line numbers. Thompson's 1928 numbering is not George's. For Tablet I the eBL edition records Thompson's 1930 column/line number for each modern line, and that concordance is used here. Where the tablet's text begins or ends inside a Thompson block, the whole block is given and the section says so. Thompson's readings reflect the state of knowledge in 1928: modern editions (George 2003; the eBL edition, updated continuously) supersede him and are not public domain, so they are not reproduced here beyond the transliteration of this tablet's signs.

English · R. Campbell Thompson (1928)

            
T iv 20Holding her unto his breast.

(’Twas thus that) Enkidu dallied

Six days, (aye) seven nights, with the courtesan-girl in his mating.


(How Enkidu was inveigled into Erech to fight with Gilgamish).


Sated at length with her charms, he turn’d his face to his cattle,

O the gazelles, (how) they scamper’d away, as soon as they saw him!


T iv 25Him, yea, Enkidu,—fled from his presence the beasts of the desert!

Enkidu losing his innocence —so, when the cattle fled from him,

Failed his knees, and he  slack’d in his running, (not) as aforetime:

Natheless he (thus) hath attain’d his full growth and hath broaden’d (his) wisdom.

English · R. Campbell Thompson (1928)

            
T v 20Enkidu, temper thine arrogance—Gilgamish, loveth him Shamash,

Anu, (and) Enlil , and Ea  have dower’d his wisdom with largesse.


(How Gilgamish dreamt of Enkidu).


(Sooth), or ever from out of thy mountains thou camest, in Erech


T v 25Gilgamish thee had beheld in a dream; so, Gilgamish coming

Spake to his mother, the dream to reveal.

'O my mother, a vision

Which I beheld in my night-time. (Behold), there were stars of the heavens,

When something like unto Anu's own self fell down on my shoulders,


T v 30(Ah, though) I heaved him, he was o’erstrong for me, (and though) his grapple

Loosed I, I was unable to shake him (from off me): (and now, all the meanwhile),

People from Erech were standing about [him , the] artisans [pressing].


T v 35On [him behind], (while) throng’d him [the heroes]; my (very) companions

Kissing [his] feet; [I, I to my breast] like a woman did hold him,

(Then) [I] presented him low at [thy] feet, [that] as mine own equal.

[Thou] might’st account him.'

[She] who knoweth all wisdom (thus) to her Seigneur she answer’d,
