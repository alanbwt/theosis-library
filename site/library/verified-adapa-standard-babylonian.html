Adapa (Standard Babylonian fragments)

        
        
Anonymous Babylonian poet (2nd millennium BCE)

        
British Museum tablet photographs + eBL manuscript transliterations (eBL corpus edition L I.7, Adapa, SB -) + English of the eBL edition (B. R. Foster (eBL edition)) · electronic Babylonian Library, Adapa, manuscript transliterations

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The later fragments of the story: Adapa as sage of Eridu, the fishing trip on which he breaks the wing of the South Wind, and the scene before Anu.
Adapa, a sage of Eridu and servant of Ea, breaks the wing of the South Wind and is summoned to heaven by Anu. On Ea's advice he refuses the bread and water offered to him, which would have given him eternal life. The story is known from a Middle Babylonian tablet found at el-Amarna in Egypt and from later fragments. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/7/SB/-) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 73 of the 97 lines of the eBL edition (75%), from 4 tablets in the British Museum: K.15072 (Nineveh), K.9994 (Nineveh), K.8743 (Nineveh), K.8214 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 24 of the 97 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: a+1-a+14 (only on MLC.1296); a+17 (only on MLC.1296); d+1-d+9 (only on MLC.1296). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.
