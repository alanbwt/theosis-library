Introduction

        
Book 13 of the Analects (論語 Lunyu), titled 子路 (Zilu) after its opening words, 30 chapters in Legge's division. It is shown on a manuscript of the Analects in the Kiyohara family collection (Seike bunko) of Kyoto University Library, two volumes in regular script, seven columns of fourteen characters to the page, main text only, with red and black Japanese reading marks. A colophon by Kiyohara Edakata dated 1550 names the copyist as his ancestor Kiyohara Yoshieda (1253-1331), which places the copy around 1300, in the late Kamakura period; the attribution is traditional. The Kiyohara were hereditary readers of the Confucian classics at the Japanese court, and their text descends from the edition of the Analects with the collected commentary of He Yan (3rd century CE): after each pair of books the scribe copied the character counts of the classic and of the commentary, although only the classic is written out. The manuscript is an Important Cultural Property of Japan.
In this copy Book 13 runs from canvas 57, left-hand page to canvas 62, left-hand page (volume 2). Each section gathers the paragraphs that begin on one page, numbered by chapter and paragraph as in Legge, and links to that page and to any page the last paragraph runs onto. The page boundaries were found by reading the first characters of every column on the photographs. The Chinese shown is the received text, from Wikisource. This manuscript differs from it in places, most often by an added or missing particle (也, 矣, 焉) or a variant character form; the differences seen at column openings are listed in the scan notes. The English is James Legge's translation of the received text.

24子貢問曰：「鄉人皆好之，何如？」子曰：「未可也。」「鄉人皆惡之，何如？」子曰：「未可也。不如鄉人之善者好之，其不善者惡之。」

            
          


          

            
English · James Legge (1861/1893)

            
21The Master said, 'Since I cannot get men pursuing the due medium, to whom I might communicate my instructions, I must find the ardent and the cautiously-decided. The ardent will advance and lay hold of truth; the cautiously-decided will keep themselves from what is wrong.'
22.1The Master said, 'The people of the south have a saying-- "A man without constancy cannot be either a wizard or a doctor." Good!
22.2'Inconstant in his virtue, he will be visited with disgrace.'
22.3The Master said, 'This arises simply from not attending to the prognostication.'
23The Master said, 'The superior man is affable, but not adulatory; the mean man is adulatory, but not affable.'
24Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'What do you say of a man who is loved by all the people of his neighborhood?' The Master replied, 'We may not for that accord our approval of him.' 'And what do you say of him who is hated by all the people of his neighborhood?' The Master said, 'We may not for that conclude that he is bad. It is better than either of these cases that the good in the neighborhood love him, and the bad hate him.'

25子曰：「君子易事而難說也；說之不以道，不說也；及其使人也，器之。小人難事而易說也。說之雖不以道，說也；及其使人也，求備焉。」

English · James Legge (1861/1893)

            
25The Master said, 'The superior man is easy to serve and difficult to please. If you try to please him in any way which is not accordant with right, he will not be pleased. But in his employment of men, he uses them according to their capacity. The mean man is difficult to serve, and easy to please. If you try to please him, though it be in a way which is not accordant with right, he may be pleased. But in his employment of men, he wishes them to be equal to everything.'
26The Master said, 'The superior man has a dignified ease without pride. The mean man has pride without a dignified ease.'
27The Master said, 'The firm, the enduring, the simple, and the modest are near to virtue.'
28Tsze-lu asked, saying, 'What qualities must a man possess to entitle him to be called a scholar?' The Master said, 'He must be thus,-- earnest, urgent, and bland:-- among his friends, earnest and urgent; among his brethren, bland.'
