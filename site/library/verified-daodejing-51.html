Introduction

        
Chapter 51 of the Daodejing (Laozi), shown on the two oldest nearly complete copies of the work: the silk manuscripts Laozi A and Laozi B, found in 1973 in Tomb 3 at Mawangdui, Changsha (Hunan), a tomb sealed in 168 BCE. Laozi A is written in a script between seal and clerical forms and does not avoid the personal name of the Han founder Liu Bang (邦); it is commonly dated before 195 BCE. Laozi B is in clerical script, avoids 邦, and is commonly dated 194-180 BCE. Both put the De part (chapters 38-81) before the Dao part (chapters 1-37), and a few chapters stand in a different order from the received text.
Each section gives the silk transcription column by column, with the Jicheng column number before each column (for Laozi B, 上 and 下 are the upper and lower halves of a column, photographed on facing plates), and links to the plate that carries those columns. □ marks a lost character; ∠, ‧ and ﹦ are marks written on the silk (﹦ repeats the character before it). Below the transcription is the received text of the Wang Bi recension, which is the text James Legge translated in 1891; the silk wording differs from it, most visibly in 恆 for received 常 (the received text avoids the name of Emperor Wen, Liu Heng), in the frequent final particle 也, and in loan characters such as 胃 for 謂.

            §Mawangdui A · Daodejing 51; Mawangdui Laozi A (甲本) col. 27 to 29; Jicheng vol. 1 plate p. 96
            

              
              
              
            

          


          
          

            
Manuscript

            
            
            
            
Where on the image
Laozi A (甲本), columns 27 to 29, on plate p. 96 of Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng, vol. 1 (column numbers are printed under each plate; the columns run from right to left). Checked on the image: column 27 reads 何故也以亓無死地焉 ‧道生之而德畜之 (the opening of this chapter, with the end of the preceding text where shown). □ marks a character lost on the silk (9 in this section). Transcription and column numbers: Wikisource 老子 (帛書甲本), which follows the Jicheng column numbering. The English is Legge's translation of the received text, shown below the silk transcription; the silk wording differs from it in many places, so compare the two Chinese texts for the variants.

          

          

          

            
Classical Chinese · Mawangdui silk transcription

                    
27‧道生之而德畜之物刑之而器成之是以萬物尊道而貴□□
28之尊德之貴也夫莫之爵而恆自祭也‧道生之畜之長之遂之亭之□之□□□□□□
29弗有也為而弗寺也長而弗宰也此之謂玄德

            
            

              Compare: Received text (Wang Bi recension), the text Legge translated
              
道生之，德畜之，物形之，勢成之。是以萬物莫不尊道而貴德。道之尊，德之貴，夫莫之命而常自然。故道生之，德畜之。長之育之，亭之毒之，蓋之覆之。生而不有，為而不恃，長而不宰。是謂玄德。

            

            
          


          

            
English · James Legge (1891)

            
1All things are produced by the Tao, and nourished by its outflowing operation. They receive their forms according to the nature of each, and are completed according to the circumstances of their condition. Therefore all things without exception honour the Tao, and exalt its outflowing operation.
2This honouring of the Tao and exalting of its operation is not the result of any ordination, but always a spontaneous tribute.
3Thus it is that the Tao produces (all things), nourishes them, brings them to their full growth, nurses them, completes them, matures them, maintains them, and overspreads them.
4It produces them and makes no claim to the possession of them; it carries them through their processes and does not vaunt its ability in doing so; it brings them to maturity and exercises no control over them;--this is called its mysterious operation.
