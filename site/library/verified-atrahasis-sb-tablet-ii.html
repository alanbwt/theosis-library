Introduction

        
The later version of the middle of the story: the noise of humankind, and Enlil's plagues, drought and famine.
Atrahasis ('Exceedingly Wise') is the Babylonian story of humanity from its creation to the Flood. The lesser gods rebel against their labour; humankind is created from clay and the flesh and blood of a slain god to do the work instead; when people multiply and their noise disturbs Enlil, he sends plague, drought and famine, and finally the Flood, which Atrahasis survives in a boat on the advice of Enki (Ea). The oldest near-complete copy was written at Sippar by the scribe Ipiq-Aya in the 17th century BCE. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/1/SB/II) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 62 of the 116 lines of the eBL edition (53%), from 5 tablets in the British Museum: BM.41330, K.6235 (Nineveh), K.6634 (Nineveh), K.17853 (Nineveh), K.14697 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 54 of the 116 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 1-29 (only on IM.124649); 44-47 (only on IM.124649); 75-84 (only on IM.124649); 92-101 (only on IM.124649); 116 (only on IM.124649, Ist-B.116). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.

Where on the image
British Museum BM.41330: obverse, the tablet's own lines 1' to 14'. eBL's corpus edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/1/SB/II) attributes SB II 30-43 to this manuscript at these column and line numbers. 42“I betook myself, I stood up and I gave the command,

43“They heard your great command.

48‘Our forced labor was heavy, our misery too much,

49‘[And so] every one of us gods

50‘[Has resolved] to do battle with Enlil!’“

51When he had heard [that] speech,

52[Then] Enlil’s tears flowed down,

53[Enlil] became disturbed at what [he] had learned,

54He said to his brother [Anu],

55Enlil became disturbed at what [he] had learned,

56He said to his brother [Anu],

57“Noble one, you should take authority off with you to heaven.

58“Take away the power, strip away your design!

59“The Anunna-gods are present before you,

60“Summon one god, [let] a new order be made.”

61Anu made ready [to speak],

62And said to the gods his brothers,

63“What do we denounce them for?

64“Their forced labor was heavy, their misery was too much.

65“Every day, as we survey the earth,

66“The forced labor was heavy and we could hear the clamor.

67“There is a task to be done.
