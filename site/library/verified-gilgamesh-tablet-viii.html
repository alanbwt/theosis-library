Introduction

        
Gilgamesh laments Enkidu, has a statue made of him, and lays out treasures and offerings for the gods of the Netherworld at his funeral.
The Standard Babylonian version of the epic, traditionally ascribed to the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni, was written on twelve tablets. Most surviving copies come from the libraries of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and from Babylon and other Babylonian cities (7th-2nd century BCE). This page gives Tablet VIII in the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition by A. R. George (2022 and later revisions).
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/VIII) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is A. R. George's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 197 of the 206 lines of the eBL edition (96%), from 8 tablets in the British Museum: K.8587 (Nineveh), K.8565 (Nineveh), BM.37189, K.6899 (Nineveh), BM.36999, K.8281 (Nineveh), BM.36909, K.19549 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 9 of the 206 lines of the eBL edition are not shown, because no photographed copy of the work used here carries them: 31-36 (no photograph in eBL: SU-1951.7 (Şanlıurfa Museum)); 47a (no photograph in eBL: SU-1951.7 (Şanlıurfa Museum)); 68 (no photograph in eBL: SU-1951.7 (Şanlıurfa Museum)); 250 (no manuscript preserves it). A line is shown only on a photographed tablet that carries it as text. Commentaries and other texts that quote a line (letters, rituals, god lists) are not used as witnesses, because they carry it with explanations or inside another text; tablets without a photograph in eBL are not used, because the line could not be shown on its tablet.

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
2Gilgameš [was mourning] for his friend:

3O Enkidu, [whom] your mother, a gazelle,

4(and) your father, a wild donkey, [created,]

5whom the wild [asses] reared with their milk,

6and the animals [of the wild taught] all the pastures!

7May the paths, O Enkidu, [of] the Cedar Forest

8[mou]rn you [without pause,] by day and by night!

9May the elders of the populous city of Uruk-the-Sheepfold mourn you!

10May the crowd who would give blessings behind us [mourn you!]

11May the high [peaks] of hills and mountains mourn you,

12[…] … pure.

13May the pastures lament like your mother!

14May [boxwood,] cypress and cedar mourn you,

15through whose midst we crept in our fury!

16May the bear mourn you, the hyena, panther, cheetah, stag and jackal,

17the lion, wild bull, deer, ibex, the herds and animals of the wild!

18May the sacred River Ulāy mourn you, along whose banks we would walk so lustily!

19May the holy Euphrates mourn you,

20which [we used] to pour in libation (as) water from skins!

21May the young men of Uruk-the-Sheepfold mourn you,

22[who] watched our battle, as we slew the Bull of Heaven!

23May the ploughman mourn you in [his furrow],

24[who] will extol your name with his sweet work-song!

25May the … of the populous city of Uruk-the-Sheepfold mourn you,

26[who] will extol your name [with] the first …!

27May the shepherd mourn you [in his cow pen,]

28[who] made sweet [for your mouth] the milk, beer and butter!

29May [the shepherd boy] mourn you […,]

30[who] used to place ghee on your lips!

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
43Hear me, O elders [of the populous city, Uruk,] hear me!

44I shall mourn Enkidu, my friend,

45like a professional mourning woman I shall lament bitterly.

46The axe at my side, in which my arm trusted,

47the sword of my belt, the shield in front of me;

48my festive garment, the girdle of my delight:

49a wicked wind has risen up against me and robbed me.

50O my friend, a mule on the run, donkey of the uplands, panther of the wild,

51my (friend) Enkidu, a mule on the run, donkey [of the uplands, panther of the wild!]

52We (it was) who joined forces and climbed the uplands,

53seized the Bull of Heaven and [killed it,]

54destroyed Humbaba, who [dwelt in the Cedar] Forest.

55Now what sleep is it that has seized [you?]

56You have become unconscious and cannot [hear me!]

57But he, he would not lift his head;

58he felt his heart, but it was not beating any more.

59He covered (his) friend, (veiling) his face like a bride,

60circling [around him] like an eagle.

61Like a lioness who is deprived of [her] cubs,

62he kept turning about, this way and that.

63He was pulling out his curly tresses and letting them fall in a heap,

64tearing off his finery and casting it away, [… like] something taboo.

65At the very first light [of dawn],

66Gilgameš sent forth a call to the land:

67Forgemaster! [Lapidary!] Coppersmith! Goldsmith! Jeweller!
