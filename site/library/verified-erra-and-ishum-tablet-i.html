Erra and Ishum, Tablet I: Erra roused to war

        
        
Kabti-ilani-Marduk (c. 8th c. BCE)

        
British Museum tablet photographs + eBL manuscript transliterations (eBL corpus edition L I.5, Erra and Ishum, SB I) + English of the eBL edition (B. R. Foster (eBL edition)) · electronic Babylonian Library, Erra and Ishum, manuscript transliterations

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
After a hymn to Ishum, the poem shows Erra lying idle. The Seven, his weapons, urge him to war. Erra goes to Babylon and persuades Marduk to leave his dwelling so that his insignia may be cleaned, promising to keep order while he is away.
Erra and Ishum, written on five tablets by Kabti-ilani-Marduk (probably 8th century BCE), tells how Erra, god of war and plague, persuades Marduk to leave his throne and then devastates Babylonia, until his vizier Ishum calms him. Copies of the poem were used as amulets against plague. The eBL edition so far covers Tablet I only. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/5/SB/I) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 134 of the 191 lines of the eBL edition (70%), from 8 tablets in the British Museum: BM.35931, BM.36197, K.8571 (Nineveh), K.16694 (Nineveh), BM.36974, BM.37217, K.10023 (Nineveh), K.13361 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 57 of the 191 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 20-27 (only on VAT.9162, SU-1951.122); 28-29 (only on VAT.9162, IM.95180, SU-1951.122, IM.74442); 30 (only on VAT.9162, IM.95180, SU-1951.122); 31-33 (only on VAT.9162, SU-1951.122); 86-91 (only on VAT.9162, SU-1951.122); 117-120 (only on SU-1951.122); 121-125 (only on SU-1951.122, VAT.10174); 126-129 (only on SU-1951.122, VAT.9162, VAT.10174); 166-176 (only on VAT.9162, IM.95180, SU-1951.122); 177-180 (only on VAT.9162, SU-1951.122); 183-191 (only on VAT.9162, SU-1951.122). English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
34He exhorted the third: “Let your face be that of a lion so that whoever beholds you melts down!”

35He said to the fourth: “May the mountain be destroyed at the attack of your fiery weapons!”

36And then to the fifth he said: “Blow like the wind and overwhelm the world!”

37To the sixth he commanded: “sweep over above and below, sparing no one!”

38The seventh he filled with viper’s venom: “Finish (all) life!”.

39Once Anu had fixed the lot of all Seven,

40He handed them over to Erra, the warrior of the gods, (saying): “Let them march at your flank!

41“When the noise of the peoples in settlements becomes unbearable to you,

42“And your temper drives you to decimate them,

43“To ravage the black-headed and the wild beasts to destroy,

44“Let these be your wrathful weapons, that they march at your flank!”.

45They are furious, their weapons are raised,

46They turn to Erra: “Rise! Stand up!

47“Why, like a worn-out old man, do you reside in a city,

48“Like a little child do you reside in a house?

49“As one who knows not the (battle) field, will we eat the bread of women?

50“As if we did not know how to fight, should we tremble and fear?

51“Going to the (battle) field is becoming for manhood, like a festival!

52“The city dweller, be he a prince, bread does not satiate him,

53“Criticized by his own people, despised,

54“He will have to beg to the humblest soldier.

55“The city dweller, however mighty his strength,

56“In what does it advantage the humblest soldier?

57“The abundant bread of the city cannot compare with the cake baked in embers!

58“The fine, honeyed beer cannot compare with the water from a waterskin!
