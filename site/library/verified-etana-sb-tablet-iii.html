Introduction

        
Etana rescues the eagle and, after a series of dreams, rides on its back towards the heaven of Anu. The text breaks off.
The Legend of Etana tells of an early king of Kish, chosen by the gods, who has no son. An eagle that betrayed its friend the serpent is thrown into a pit; Etana rescues it, and the eagle carries him up towards heaven to find the plant of birth. The story is known from Old Babylonian, Middle Assyrian and Standard Babylonian versions; the end is lost. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/9/SB/III) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 131 of the 131 lines of the eBL edition (100%), from 5 tablets in the British Museum: K.2606 (Nineveh), K.3651 (Nineveh), K.19530 (Nineveh), 1882,0323.6 (Nineveh), K.8563 (Nineveh).

b+15The eagle [said] to him, to Etana,

b+16“My friend, [your] dre[ams] are obvious!

b+17“Come, let me take you up to heaven [of Anu].

b+18“Put [your chest] against my chest,

b+19“Put [your hands] against my wing feathers,

b+21He put [his chest] against his chest,

b+22He put [his] hands against his wing feathers,

b+23He put [his] hands against his sides,

b+28“Examine the sea, look for its boundaries.”

b+35When he had borne him aloft a third league,
