Introduction

        
Hesiod's Works and Days (c. 700 BCE) is a poem of advice to his brother Perses on justice, work and the farmer's year. It contains the myth of Prometheus and Pandora and the Five Ages of mankind, from the golden race to the race of iron. This part: Friends and rules of conduct (lines 706-764, 59 lines). Advice on friends and guests, and a list of religious and everyday rules.
The page photographs are from Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 32.16 (paper, late 13th century; a large collection of Greek poetry that also holds letters of Maximus Planudes), Works and Days on fols. 233r-239v, in the Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana's Teca Digitale. Older manuscripts of the poem exist (Paris gr. 2771, 10th/11th century; Florence, Laur. Plut. 31.39, 12th century), but this copy is legible on every page and holds Hesiod's three poems together. This part is on fol. 238v, 239r. The scribe writes two verses to a row, one in each column, so a page is read across the columns, row by row; the margins are filled with scholia (ancient and Byzantine notes). Each section below is one page; its note gives the page's first and last verse as read on the photograph.
The Greek is Evelyn-White's Loeb text (1914) via Perseus, not a transcription of this manuscript, whose wording differs here and there. Verses the editor brackets as later additions are shown in square brackets. The English is Evelyn-White's prose translation (1914); it is cut at the end of his sentences, so where a sentence runs across a page break it is given whole to one page; each section's note gives the exact lines its English covers.

English · H. G. Evelyn-White (1914)

            
706Be careful to avoid the anger of the deathless gods. Do not make a friend equal to a brother; but if you do, do not wrong him first, and do not lie to please the tongue. But if he wrongs you first, 710offending either in word or in deed, remember to repay him double; but if he asks you to be his friend again and be ready to give you satisfaction, welcome him. He is a worthless man who makes now one and now another his friend; but as for you, do not let your face put your heart to shame. 715Do not get a name either as lavish or as churlish, as a friend of rogues, or as a slanderer of good men. Never dare to taunt a man with deadly poverty which eats out the heart; it is sent by the deathless gods. 720The best treasure a man can have is a sparing tongue, and the greatest pleasure, one that moves orderly; for if you speak evil, you yourself will soon be worse spoken of. Do not be boorish at a common feast where there are many guests; the pleasure is greatest and the expense is least.

English · H. G. Evelyn-White (1914)

            
724Never pour a libation of sparkling wine to Zeus after dawn 725with unwashed hands, nor to others of the deathless gods; otherwise they do not hear your prayers but spit them back. Do not stand upright facing the sun when you make water, but remember to do this when he has set and towards his rising. And do not make water as you go, whether on the road or off the road, 730and do not uncover yourself: the nights belong to the blessed gods. A scrupulous man who has a wise heart sits down or goes to the wall of an enclosed court. Do not expose yourself befouled by the fireside in your house, but avoid this. 735Do not beget children when you are come back from ill-omened burial, but after a festival of the gods. 737Never cross the sweet-flowing water of ever-rolling rivers afoot until you have prayed, gazing into the soft flood, and washed your hands in the clear, lovely water. 740Whoever crosses a river with hands unwashed of wickedness, the gods are angry with him and bring trouble upon him afterwards. At a cheerful festival of the gods do not cut the withered from the quick upon that which has five branches with bright steel. Never put the ladle upon the mixing-bowl 745at a wine party, for malignant ill-luck is attached to that. When you are building a house, do not leave it rough hewn, or a cawing crow may settle on it and croak. Take nothing to eat or to wash with from uncharmed pots, for in them there is mischief. 750Do not let a boy of twelve years sit on things which may not be moved, for that is bad, and makes a man unmanly; nor yet a child of twelve months, for that has the same effect. A man should not clean his body with water in which a woman has washed, for there is bitter mischief in that also for a time. 755When you come upon a burning sacrifice, do not make a mock of mysteries, for Heaven is angry at this also. Never make water in the mouths of rivers which flow to the sea, nor yet in springs; but be careful to avoid this. And do not ease yourself in them: it is not well to do this. 760So do: and avoid the talk of men. For Talk is mischievous, light, and easily raised, but hard to bear and difficult to be rid of. Talk never wholly dies away when many people voice her: even Talk is in some ways divine.
