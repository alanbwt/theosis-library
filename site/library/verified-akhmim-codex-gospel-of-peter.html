Gospel of Peter (2nd c. AD; Akhmim codex, 6th-9th c.)

        
Akhmim codex (P. Cair. 10759) pages 2-10 in A. Lods's heliogravure plates (1893, Internet Archive) + Lods's diplomatic transcription + English of J. Armitage Robinson (Ante-Nicene Fathers IX, public domain) · A. Lods, diplomatic transcription and heliogravure facsimile (1893)

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The Gospel of Peter was known only by name, from Eusebius and from Serapion of Antioch (c. 200 AD), until a long fragment of it was found in the winter of 1886-87 in a monk's grave at Akhmim (ancient Panopolis) in Upper Egypt. The fragment tells the story of the passion from Pilate's hand-washing to the resurrection, in the first person of Simon Peter, and breaks off as Peter, Andrew and Levi go to the sea. It is now generally dated to the 2nd century.
The manuscript. The Akhmim codex (Cairo, Egyptian Museum, P. Cair. 10759) is a small parchment book of 33 leaves; proposed dates for its hands range from the 6th to the 9th century. Page 1 carries a cross; the Gospel of Peter fills pages 2-10 and ends with a braided band; the Apocalypse of Peter and the Greek text of 1 Enoch 1-32 follow. The pages shown are A. Lods's heliogravure plates of 1893, made from photographs taken in the Gizeh Museum: each plate reproduced two pages, and each page has been cut out of its plate and turned upright here.
Texts and mapping. The Greek is Lods's line-by-line diplomatic transcription of the same pages, with his page and line numbers (shown as page.line), so every line can be found on the photograph. Each section is one chapter of J. Armitage Robinson's division, which is also the division of his English translation; the chapter openings were located in Lods's lines by their Greek words. Robinson revised his translation against the photographic facsimile.

12 And at dawn upon the Lord’s day Mary Magdalen, a disciple of the Lord, fearing because of the Jews, since they were burning with wrath, had not done at the Lord’s sepulchre the things which women are wont to do for those that die and for those that are beloved by them—she took her friends with her and came to the sepulchre where he was laid. And they feared lest the Jews should see them, and they said, Although on that day on which he was crucified we could not weep and lament, yet now let us do these things at his sepulchre. For the stone was great, and we fear lest some one see us.
