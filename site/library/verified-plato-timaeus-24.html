Introduction

        
Stephanus page 24 of Plato's Timaeus (24a-24e): the laws of ancient Athens compared with Egypt's; the power from the Atlantic Ocean.



Timaeus 20d-25d is Critias' first account of Atlantis: the story Solon was told by a priest at Sais in Egypt about an Athens of 9,000 years before, which defeated an invading power from the island of Atlantis beyond the Pillars of Heracles, after which the island sank into the sea. Plato returns to it at length in the Critias.



The manuscript is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, grec 1807, known to editors as Plato A. It was copied in Constantinople around 850-875 by the scribe of the so-called philosophical collection, and is the oldest surviving medieval manuscript of the Timaeus and Critias (tetralogies VIII-IX: Clitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias, Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters). Each page has two columns of 44 lines. The Timaeus fills fol. 114r-144v and the Critias fol. 145r-151r.



On the manuscript this page of text runs over fol. 116v col. b to fol. 117r col. b. Each section below is linked to the folio side(s) that carry it. The mapping comes from reading the first words of every column on the images and finding them in Burnet's text: fol. 116v col. b begins ‹ἐξ ὧν σύ τε καὶ πᾶσα› (Tim. 23b); fol. 117r col. a begins ‹σκόπει πρὸς τοὺς τῇδε· πολλὰ γὰρ› (Tim. 24a); fol. 117r col. b begins ‹-εξαμένη πρῶτον κατῴκισεν. ᾠκεῖτε δὴ οὖν› (Tim. 24d).



The Greek is John Burnet's critical text (Oxford, 1902), not a transcription of this manuscript; the scribe's spelling, accents and occasional readings differ from it. The English is R. G. Bury's Loeb translation (1929).

Greek · Burnet

            
πάντων ἐφεξῆς εἰς αὖθις κατὰ σχολὴν αὐτὰ τὰ γράμματα λαβόντες διέξιμεν. τοὺς μὲν οὖν νόμους σκόπει πρὸς τοὺς τῇδε· πολλὰ γὰρ παραδείγματα τῶν τότε παρʼ ὑμῖν ὄντων ἐνθάδε νῦν ἀνευρήσεις, πρῶτον μὲν τὸ τῶν ἱερέων γένος ἀπὸ τῶν ἄλλων χωρὶς ἀφωρισμένον, μετὰ δὲ τοῦτο τὸ τῶν δημιουργῶν, ὅτι καθʼ αὑτὸ ἕκαστον ἄλλῳ δὲ οὐκ ἐπιμειγνύμενον δημιουργεῖ, τό τε τῶν νομέων καὶ τὸ τῶν θηρευτῶν τό τε

            
          


          

            
English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
the full account in precise order and detail we shall go through later at our leisure, taking the actual writings. To get a view of their laws, look at the laws here; for you will find existing here at the present time many examples of the laws which then existed in your city. You see, first, how the priestly class is separated off from the rest; next, the class of craftsmen, of which each sort works by itself without mixing with any other; then the classes of shepherds, hunters, and farmers, each distinct and separate.
