Great Hymn to Shamash (Babylonian hymn to the sun god)

        
        
Anonymous Babylonian poet (late 2nd millennium BCE)

        
British Museum tablet photographs + eBL manuscript transliterations (eBL corpus edition L III.4, Great Prayer to Shamash (ed. G. Rozzi), SB -) + English of the eBL edition (B. R. Foster (eBL edition)) · electronic Babylonian Library, Great Prayer to Shamash (ed. G. Rozzi), manuscript transliterations

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The hymn praises Shamash, whose light illuminates heaven and earth, who watches over all peoples and creatures, judges the dishonest judge, the false merchant and the oppressor, rewards the honest, and hears the prayers of the weak and of travellers on land and sea.
The Great Hymn to Shamash is a Babylonian hymn of about 200 lines to the sun god as the judge who sees everything under heaven and the protector of travellers, merchants, the poor and the oppressed. It survives mainly on copies from the libraries of Nineveh and from Babylonian cities. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/3/4/SB/-) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so (BM.36296). The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 200 of the 200 lines of the eBL edition (100%), from 7 tablets in the British Museum: BM.36296, K.3182 (Nineveh), Sm.1033 (Nineveh), K.3474 (Nineveh), K.3650 (Nineveh), BM.38849, 1883,0118.472 (Nineveh).
