The Babylonian Theodicy (dialogue of the sufferer and his friend)

        
        
Saggil-kinam-ubbib (c. 1000 BCE)

        
British Museum tablet photographs + eBL manuscript transliterations (eBL corpus edition L II.1, Theodicy, SB -) + English of the eBL edition (B. R. Foster (eBL edition)) · electronic Babylonian Library, Theodicy, manuscript transliterations

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The sufferer complains that he is an orphan, poor and oppressed while the wicked prosper; his friend answers that the ways of the gods are unknowable and that the pious are rewarded in the end. At the close the friend concedes that the gods made humans prone to falsehood, and the sufferer asks for mercy from his god and goddess.
The Babylonian Theodicy is a dialogue in 27 stanzas of 11 lines between a sufferer and his friend about the injustice of the world. Each stanza begins with the same sign, and the initial signs together form an acrostic that names the author, Saggil-kinam-ubbib, an incantation priest, as a worshipper of god and king. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/2/1/SB/-) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.

English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
1[O my] sagacious [brother], come, let me tell you,

2The distress [I have seen, time and again], I would recount to you.

3Such deep misery [as has befallen me, may it weigh heavy] on your feelings,

4[In …] most distressful, I will always sing your praises:

5Where be the man of insight so able to advise?

6Whence the man of knowledge comparable to you?

7Where else to such a counsellor my woe could I relate?

8I was at a loss when heartache came upon me,

9I was the youngest child when fate carried off him who begot me,

10She who brought me into the world departed to the land of no return,

11My father and mother left me with no one to care for me!

12Considerate friend, what you tell is a sorrowful tale,

13My dear friend, you have let your creativity harbor ill.

14You make your estimable discretion feeble-minded,

15You alter your bright expression to a scowl.

16Our fathers are given up that they go death’s way,

17They will cross the river Hubur, as is commanded from of old.

18When you survey teeming humankind all around,

19If the poor man’s son is thriving, someone helped him prosper,

20Who did favors for the sleek and wealthy?

21He who looks to his god has a protector,

22The diffident man who reveres his goddess will garner wealth.

23My friend, your heart is a wellspring of depth unplumbed,

24The upsurging swell of the ocean that brooks no diminishment.

25To you, then, let me pose my questions, learn my drift,

26Hearken to me but for a moment, hear my declaration.

27Want and craving make up the covering of my body,

28I have outlived my success, (my) prime is past,

29(My) energies are gone feeble, (my) prospering is over,

30Moaning and woe have clouded [my] features.

31The sustenance from my tilth nowhere near suffices [me],

32Of the fruit of the vine, what sustains humankind, I am far from having enough.

34My well-thought-out speech is the peak of good advice,

46[I have taken to heart the] honeyed discourse of [your] lips.
