Introduction

        
Book 21 of the Iliad (611 lines in Monro and Allen's edition) as written in Venetus A (Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, Marc. gr. Z. 454 = 822), the oldest complete manuscript of the Iliad, copied in the 10th century with the text in the centre of each page and the scholia around it. This book is on fol. 270r-282r; each section below is one page of the manuscript and links that page.
The page of every line comes from the Homer Multitext Project's index of the manuscript (release 2024c), which records where each line stands on the page photographs.
Greek: the manuscript's own text, from the Homer Multitext diplomatic edition of Venetus A, with its accents, punctuation and signs as given there. The Oxford text of Monro and Allen (1920) is given for comparison.
English: A. T. Murray's translation (Loeb Classical Library, 1924). Murray numbers every fifth line and starts a new paragraph at many other lines; the English is divided only at those points, and a passage that runs across a page break goes with the page that holds most of its lines. The English of a section can therefore begin or end a few lines away from the Greek; each section gives its exact English line range.
Not in Venetus A: lines 21.158, 434, 480, 510 of the standard text are not in this manuscript. They are shown in the Monro and Allen text and in the English of the page where they would stand.

English · A. T. Murray (1924)

            
1But when they were now come to the ford of the fair-flowing river, even eddying Xanthus that immortal Zeus begat, there Achilles cleft them asunder, and the one part he drave to the plain toward the city, even where the Achaeans were fleeing in rout 5the day before, what time glorious Hector was raging—thitherward poured forth some in rout, and Hera spread before them a thick mist to hinder them; but the half of them were pent into the deep-flowing river with its silver eddies. Therein they flung themselves with a great din, and the sheer-falling streams resounded, 10and the banks round about rang loudly; and with noise of shouting swam they this way and that, whirled about in the eddies. And as when beneath the onrush of fire locusts take wing to flee unto a river, and the unwearied fire burneth them with its sudden oncoming, and they shrink down into the water; 15even so before Achilles was the sounding stream of deep-eddying Xanthus filled confusedly with chariots and with men. But the Zeus-begotten left there his spear upon the bank, leaning against the tamarisk bushes, and himself leapt in like a god with naught but his sword; and grim was the work he purposed in his heart, and turning him this way 20and that he smote and smote; and from them uprose hideous groaning as they were smitten with the sword, and the water grew red with blood. And as before a dolphin, huge of maw, other fishes flee and fill the nooks of some harbour of fair anchorage in their terror, for greedily doth he devour whatsoever one he catcheth;

Murray marks every fifth line and each paragraph; a block that crosses the page break goes with the page holding most of its lines.

          

          

          

            
Greek · Venetus A (Homer Multitext)

                    
605ὡς αἰεὶ ἔλποιτο κιχήσεσθαι ποσὶν οἷσι:
606τόφρ' ἄλλοι Τρῶες πεφοβημένοι ἦλθον, ὁμίλῳ
607ἀσπάσιοι προτὶ ἄστυ. πόλις δ' ἔμπλητο ἀλέντων:
608οὐδ' ἄρα τοί γ' ἔτλαν πόλιος καὶ τείχεος ἐκτὸς
609μεῖναι ἔτ' ἀλλήλους, καὶ γνώμεναι, ὅς τε πεφεύγοι
610ὅς τ' ἔθαν', ἐν πολέμῳ: ἂλλ' ἐσσυμένως ἐσέχυντο

English · A. T. Murray (1924)

            
605that he ever hoped to overtake him in his running—meanwhile the rest of the Trojans that were fleeing in rout came crowding gladly toward the city, and the town was filled with the throng of them. Neither dared they longer to await one another outside the city and wall, and to know who perchance was escaped and 610who had been slain in the fight; but with eager haste they poured into the city, whomsoever of them his feet and knees might save.
