Introduction

        
Chapter 49 of the Daodejing (Laozi), shown on the two oldest nearly complete copies of the work: the silk manuscripts Laozi A and Laozi B, found in 1973 in Tomb 3 at Mawangdui, Changsha (Hunan), a tomb sealed in 168 BCE. Laozi A is written in a script between seal and clerical forms and does not avoid the personal name of the Han founder Liu Bang (邦); it is commonly dated before 195 BCE. Laozi B is in clerical script, avoids 邦, and is commonly dated 194-180 BCE. Both put the De part (chapters 38-81) before the Dao part (chapters 1-37), and a few chapters stand in a different order from the received text.
Each section gives the silk transcription column by column, with the Jicheng column number before each column (for Laozi B, 上 and 下 are the upper and lower halves of a column, photographed on facing plates), and links to the plate that carries those columns. □ marks a lost character; ∠, ‧ and ﹦ are marks written on the silk (﹦ repeats the character before it). Below the transcription is the received text of the Wang Bi recension, which is the text James Legge translated in 1891; the silk wording differs from it, most visibly in 恆 for received 常 (the received text avoids the name of Emperor Wen, Liu Heng), in the frequent final particle 也, and in loan characters such as 胃 for 謂.

            §Mawangdui A · Daodejing 49; Mawangdui Laozi A (甲本) col. 22 to 24; Jicheng vol. 1 plate p. 96
            

              
              
              
            

          


          
          

            
Manuscript

            
            
            
            
Where on the image
Laozi A (甲本), columns 22 to 24, on plate p. 96 of Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng, vol. 1 (column numbers are printed under each plate; the columns run from right to left). Checked on the image: column 24 reads 之在天下歙﹦焉為天下渾心百姓皆屬. The opening characters of this chapter are lost or not readable on this plate image, so these are the first characters that were confirmed. Column 22 was located on the image at the edge of plate p. 96; the Wikisource page split puts it on the neighbouring plate. □ marks a character lost on the silk (24 in this section). Transcription and column numbers: Wikisource 老子 (帛書甲本), which follows the Jicheng column numbering. The English is Legge's translation of the received text, shown below the silk transcription; the silk wording differs from it in many places, so compare the two Chinese texts for the variants.

          

          

          

            
Classical Chinese · Mawangdui silk transcription

                    
22□□□□
23□以百□之心為□善者善之不善者亦善□□□□□□□□□□□□□□
24□信也□□之在天下歙﹦焉為天下渾心百姓皆屬耳目焉聖人皆咳之

            
            

              Compare: Received text (Wang Bi recension), the text Legge translated
              
聖人無常心，以百姓心為心。善者，吾善之；不善者，吾亦善之，德善。信者，吾信之；不信者，吾亦信之，德信。聖人在天下歙歙，為天下渾其心，百姓皆注其耳目,聖人皆孩之。

            

            
          


          

            
English · James Legge (1891)

            
1The sage has no invariable mind of his own; he makes the mind of the people his mind.
2To those who are good (to me), I am good; and to those who are not good (to me), I am also good;--and thus (all) get to be good. To those who are sincere (with me), I am sincere; and to those who are not sincere (with me), I am also sincere;--and thus (all) get to be sincere.
3The sage has in the world an appearance of indecision, and keeps his mind in a state of indifference to all. The people all keep their eyes and ears directed to him, and he deals with them all as his children.
