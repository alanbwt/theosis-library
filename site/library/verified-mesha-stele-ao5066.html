Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone), Louvre AO 5066

        
        
Mesha, king of Moab (c. 840 BCE)

        
Louvre AO 5066 photograph (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0) + H. F. B. Compston's 1919 text and translation (public domain) · H. F. B. Compston, The Inscription on the Stele of Mesa (1919)

        
      


      

      

        
Introduction

        
The Mesha Stele, or Moabite Stone, is a black basalt stele set up about 840 BCE by Mesha, king of Moab, at Dibon (Dhiban in Jordan). In 34 lines of Moabite, written in the old Hebrew-Phoenician alphabet, Mesha says that Omri, king of Israel, and his son oppressed Moab because the god Chemosh was angry with his land, and that Chemosh then gave Mesha victory: he took Ataroth, Nebo and Jahaz from Israel and dragged 'the vessels of YHWH' before Chemosh, and he rebuilt the towns of his kingdom. It is the longest Iron Age inscription from the lands east of the Jordan and names Omri, Israel and the God of Israel. Its story runs parallel to the account of Mesha's revolt in the Old Testament, 2 Kings 3:4-27.
The stele was seen by the missionary F. A. Klein in 1868. Before it could be removed, a paper squeeze was taken for Charles Clermont-Ganneau, and in 1869 the stone was broken up by the local people. The larger pieces were recovered and are now in the Louvre (AO 5066), set in a plaster restoration based on the squeeze.
The text is H. F. B. Compston's edition of 1919 in Hebrew square letters, with his line-by-line English translation. Square brackets mark his restorations, overlined letters are uncertain, and dots in the English mark words lost at the broken end of the stele. Each section groups a few lines and shows the photograph of the whole inscribed face.
