Introduction

        
Before heaven and earth were named, Apsu and Tiamat mingled their waters and the gods were born. Disturbed by their noise, Apsu plans to destroy them; Ea puts him to sleep and kills him, and in his dwelling Marduk is born. Tiamat, urged on by other gods, creates monsters for battle and gives Qingu the Tablet of Destinies.
Enuma Elish ('When on high'), the Babylonian Poem of Creation, tells how Marduk, god of Babylon, defeated Tiamat, created the ordered world and became king of the gods. It was written on seven tablets and recited in Babylon during the New Year festival. The date of composition is debated (proposals range from the 18th to the 12th century BCE); the surviving copies date from the 9th to the 2nd century BCE and come from Ashur, Nineveh, Kish, Babylon, Sippar, Uruk and other sites. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/2/SB/I) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so (BM.36681, BM.36726, BM.37287, BM.37969). The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 162 of the 162 lines of the eBL edition (100%), from 12 tablets in the British Museum: K.5419.C (Nineveh), BM.45528, BM.36726, BM.36681, 1881,0727.80 (Nineveh), BM.46803, K.13299 (Nineveh), BM.37287, Rm.982 (Nineveh), BM.45571, BM.38024, BM.37969.

The English of these lines translates eBL's composite text, so it includes words that are not preserved on this tablet but are restored from other manuscripts or by the editors. Square brackets in the English mark eBL's restorations in its composite text; words that survive on other tablets carry no brackets even where this tablet is broken.

English · B. R. Foster (eBL edition)

                    
155“You are the greatest, my husband, you are illustrious,

156“Your command shall always be greatest, over all the Anunna-gods.”

157She gave him the tablet of destinies, had him hold it to his chest, (saying)

158“As for you, your command shall not be changed, your utterance shall endure.”

159When Qingu was the highest, had taken supremacy,

160He ordained destinies for the gods her children,

161“Whatever you (gods) command shall cause a blaze to die out,

162“Your concentrated venom shall make a mighty one yield.”
