Introduction

        
The Phaedo tells of Socrates' last conversation in prison before he drank the hemlock. In this passage (the 'affinity argument') he divides things into two kinds: the invisible, which is always the same, and the visible, which never stays the same. The soul is more like the first, the body like the second; and 'the soul is most like the divine and immortal and intellectual and uniform and indissoluble and ever unchanging' (80b). A soul that has lived by philosophy, keeping itself pure of the body, goes away at death 'into that which is like itself, into the invisible, divine, immortal, and wise' (81a), while souls bound to the body wander and are born again; philosophy is described as the release of the soul.



The pages shown are from the Clarke Plato (Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS. E. D. Clarke 39), copied in Constantinople in 895, the oldest manuscript of the Phaedo (siglum B). The passage runs from fol. 37r to the top of fol. 40v, eight pages. On fol. 38v the inner part of the first lines is damaged and repaired, and some letters there have been retraced by a later hand. Greek · Burnet

                    
ἄλλο σῶμα καὶ ὥσπερ σπειρομένη ἐμφύεσθαι, καὶ ἐκ τούτων ἄμοιρος εἶναι τῆς τοῦ θείου τε καὶ καθαροῦ καὶ μονοειδοῦς συνουσίας. ἀληθέστατα, ἔφη, λέγεις, ὁ Κέβης, ὦ Σώκρατες. τούτων τοίνυν ἕνεκα, ὦ Κέβης, οἱ δικαίως φιλομαθεῖς κόσμιοί εἰσι καὶ ἀνδρεῖοι, οὐχ ὧν οἱ πολλοὶ ἕνεκά φασιν· ἢ σὺ οἴει;

like seed that is sown. Therefore it has no part in the communion with the divine and pure and absolute. What you say, Socrates, is very true, said Cebes.
