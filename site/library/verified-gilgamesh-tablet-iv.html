Introduction

        
On the way, at each stage, the heroes perform a ritual for dreams, and Gilgamesh has a series of frightening dreams that Enkidu interprets favourably. They reach the edge of the forest.
The Standard Babylonian version of the epic, traditionally ascribed to the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni, was written on twelve tablets. Most surviving copies come from the libraries of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and from Babylon and other Babylonian cities (7th-2nd century BCE). This page gives Tablet IV in the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition by A. R. George (2022 and later revisions).
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/IV) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed manuscript with the most signs preserved there. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives. Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is A. R. George's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 81 of the 175 lines of the eBL edition (46%), from 7 tablets in the British Museum: K.8586 (Nineveh), Rm.853, K.10777 (Nineveh), Sm.1040 (Nineveh), BM.45883, 1879,0708.342 (Nineveh), K.7224 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 94 of the 175 lines of the eBL edition have no witness here with an open photograph: 1-5 (only on VAT.14512); 6-21 (no manuscript preserves it); 44-55 (no manuscript preserves it); 69-75 (only on BM.93052); 78-83 (no manuscript preserves it); a+1'-a+9' (only on BM.93052); 130-142 (no manuscript preserves it); 155-157 (only on VAT.14513); 158 (only on VAT.14513, Private.1); 165 (only on VAT.14513, Private.1); 166-176 (only on VAT.14513); 177-183 (no manuscript preserves it); c+1' (only on VAT.14512); c+2' (only on Private.1); d+1' (only on VAT.14512). Lines that survive only on tablets whose photographs are not openly licensed (for example in the Iraq Museum, Berlin, Istanbul, Ankara, Philadelphia or private collections), or only on tablets whose eBL photograph does not show the lines, are omitted.

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
87‘[O mountain], bring me a dream, so I may see [a favourable message from Šamaš!]’

88[Enkidu] made for him [a] ‘house of Zaqīqu’,

89[he fixed] a storm-door in its doorway.

90[in the circle he had] drawn he made him lie down,

91[and falling flat] like a net lay himself in the doorway.

92Gilgamesh rested his chin on his knees,

93the sleep that spills over people fell upon him.

94[In the] middle watch (of the night) he reached his sleep’s end,

95he arose to talk to his friend:

96‘My friend, did you not call me? Why am I awake?

97Did you not touch me? Why am I in confusion?

98Did a god not pass by? Why is my flesh benumbed?

99My friend, I have seen a third dream,

100and the dream that I saw was completely confused.

101The heavens cried aloud, while the earth was rumbling,

102the day grew still, darkness went forth.

103Lightning flashed down, fire broke out,

104[flames] kept flaring up, death kept raining down.

105The fire so bright dimmed and went out,

106[the flames] diminished and turned into cinders.

107[You were] born in the wild, can we take counsel?’

108Enkidu [heard the words of his friend,] making his dream meaningful to him, he said to Gilgameš:

109‘[My friend,] your dream is good, [its message] is lucky.’
