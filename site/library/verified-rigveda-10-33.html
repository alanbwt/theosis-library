Introduction

        
Rigveda 10.33 is a hymn of 9 verses addressed to a singer's lament. It stands in book (maṇḍala) 10, the last and latest book. The Rigveda is the oldest collection of Sanskrit hymns, composed in the second half of the second millennium BCE and handed down orally for many centuries before it was written down.
The manuscript shown is Penn Ms. Coll. 390 Item 41 (University of Pennsylvania), an undated paper manuscript of the padapāṭha (the word-by-word recitation text, in which every word is written separately and compounds are split) of the whole Ṛgveda in Devanāgarī, 884 leaves in fascicles, 7-9 lines to the page, accents in red; this hymn stands on fol. 772r-772v. Each section below gives the verses that end on one page; the page boundaries were read on the images (the opening words of every page), not counted out. Where the manuscript is a padapāṭha (the word-by-word recitation text), the section shows Aufrecht's continuous (saṃhitā) text first and, as the edition text, the padapāṭha of the same verses, which is the form written on the page.

Where on the image
Penn Ms. Coll. 390 Item 41, fol. 772r (numbered 88r by the scribe) (OPenn image 772, lower leaf). Checked on the image: the Rigveda text on this page begins in RV 10.33.1 ‹pra mā yuyujre pra-yujaḥ›. Lines 1-7 carry the anukramaṇī for the next adhyāya (number 56) and ॥ hariḥ oṃ ॥; 10.33.1 (pra mā yuyujre prayujaḥ janānām) begins in line 7. The next page with Rigveda text, fol. 772v (numbered 88v by the scribe), begins in RV 10.33.2 ‹nagnatā jasuḥ veḥ na›. Verse 10.33.1 ends on this page.

English · Ralph T. H. Griffith (1889-1897)

                    
1THE urgings of the people have impelled me, and by,the nearest way I bring you Pûshan. The Universal Gods have brought me safely. The cry was heard, Behold, Dubsasu cometh!

English · Ralph T. H. Griffith (1889-1897)

                    
2The ribs that compass me give pain and trouble me like rival wives. Indigence, nakedness, exhaustion press me sore: my mind is fluttering like a bird's.
3As rats eat weavers' threads, cares are consuming me, thy singer, Ṣatakratu, me. Have mercy on us once, O Indra, Bounteous Lord: be thou a Father unto us.
4I the priests' R̥ishi chose as prince most liberal Kuruṣravaṇa, The son of Trasadasyu's son,
5Whose three bays harnessed to the car bear me straight onward: I will laud The giver of a thousand meeds,
6The sire of Upamaṣravas, even him whose words were passing sweet, As a fair field is to its lord.
7Mark, Upamaṣravas, his son, mark, grandson of Mitrâtithi: I am thy father's eulogist.
8If I controlled Immortal Gods, yea, even were I Lord of men, My liberal prince were living still.
9None lives, even had he hundred lives, beyond the statute of the Gods So am I parted from my friend.
