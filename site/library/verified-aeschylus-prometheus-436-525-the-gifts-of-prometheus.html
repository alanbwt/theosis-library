Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 436-525: the arts Prometheus gave to mankind

Introduction

        
Prometheus Bound, handed down under the name of Aeschylus (died 456 BCE), shows the Titan Prometheus nailed to a rock at the edge of the world as punishment for stealing fire and giving it to mortals. In this speech to the chorus of the daughters of Oceanus he describes the first state of mankind: they saw without seeing and heard without understanding, 'like to shapes in dreams', and lived in sunless caves like ants, with no way of telling the seasons. Prometheus taught them the risings and settings of the stars, numbers, 'chiefest of sciences', and the combining of letters, the yoke for animals, the chariot and the ship, medicine, every kind of divination, and the use of the metals hidden in the earth: 'every art possessed by man comes from Prometheus' (506). The section ends with a short exchange in which Prometheus says that art is weaker than Necessity, which is steered by the Fates and the Furies, and hints at the secret on which the fate of Zeus depends.



The pages shown are from Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 32.9, the Medicean manuscript, written in the 10th century, the oldest and most important manuscript of Aeschylus (siglum M). The play begins on fol. 147v; lines 436-525 stand on fol. 152v, 153r and 153v, in one column of verse with ancient commentary (scholia) in the margins. Each section below is one speech or one part of a speech; the page or pages listed for it were identified by reading the first and last verse of each page on the images. The Greek is Smyth's edition (1922), not a transcription of the manuscript. The English is Smyth's Loeb translation of the same year. The rest of the play is not included in this entry.

English · H. W. Smyth (1922)

            
Prometheus

436Nay, impute it not to pride nor yet to wilfulness that I am silent. Painful thoughts devour my heart as I behold myself maltreated thus. And yet who but I definitely assigned their prerogatives unto these upstart gods? But of this I speak not; for my tale would tell you naught save what ye know. But hearken to the miseries that beset mankind—how that they were witless erst and I made them to have sense and be endowed with reason. Nor will I speak to upbraid mankind, but to set forth the friendly purpose that inspired my boons.

Chorus

507Do not then benefit mortals beyond due measure and yet be heedless of thine own distress; forasmuch as I am of good hope that thou shalt yet be loosed from these bonds and have power no wise inferior to Zeus.


Prometheus

Not thus, nor yet, is fulfilling Fate destined to bring this end to pass. When I have been bent by pangs and tortures infinite, thus only am I to escape my bondage. Art is feebler far than Necessity.


Chorus

Who then is the steersman of Necessity?


Prometheus

The triform Fates and mindful Furies.


Chorus

Can it be that Zeus hath lesser power than they?


Prometheus

Aye, in that at least he cannot escape what is foredoomed.


Chorus

Why, what is foredoomed for Zeus save to hold eternal sway?


Prometheus

This thou must not learn as yet; be not importunate.


Chorus

'Tis some solemn secret, surely, that thou dost enshroud in mystery.


Prometheus

Bethink ye of some other theme, for 'tis in no wise meet time to discourse of this. At every hazard this must be kept concealed; for 'tis by safeguarding it that I am to escape my ignominious bonds and outrage.
