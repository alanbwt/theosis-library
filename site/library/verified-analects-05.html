Introduction

        
Book 5 of the Analects (論語 Lunyu), titled 公冶長 (Gongye Chang) after its opening words, 27 chapters in Legge's division. It is shown on a manuscript of the Analects in the Kiyohara family collection (Seike bunko) of Kyoto University Library, two volumes in regular script, seven columns of fourteen characters to the page, main text only, with red and black Japanese reading marks. A colophon by Kiyohara Edakata dated 1550 names the copyist as his ancestor Kiyohara Yoshieda (1253-1331), which places the copy around 1300, in the late Kamakura period; the attribution is traditional. The Kiyohara were hereditary readers of the Confucian classics at the Japanese court, and their text descends from the edition of the Analects with the collected commentary of He Yan (3rd century CE): after each pair of books the scribe copied the character counts of the classic and of the commentary, although only the classic is written out. The manuscript is an Important Cultural Property of Japan.
In this copy Book 5 runs from canvas 18, right-hand page to canvas 22, right-hand page (volume 1). Each section gathers the paragraphs that begin on one page, numbered by chapter and paragraph as in Legge, and links to that page and to any page the last paragraph runs onto. The page boundaries were found by reading the first characters of every column on the photographs. The Chinese shown is the received text, from Wikisource. This manuscript differs from it in places, most often by an added or missing particle (也, 矣, 焉) or a variant character form; the differences seen at column openings are listed in the scan notes. The English is James Legge's translation of the received text.

Classical Chinese · received text

            
1.1子謂公冶長，「可妻也；雖在縲絏之中，非其罪也。」以其子妻之。
1.2子謂南容，「邦有道，不廢；邦無道，免於刑戮。」以其兄之子妻之。
2子謂子賤：「君子哉若人！魯無君子者，斯焉取斯？」
3子貢問曰：「賜也何如？」子曰：「女器也」。曰：「何器也？」曰：「瑚璉也。」
4.1或曰：「雍也，仁而不佞。」
4.2子曰：「焉用佞？禦人以口給，屢憎於人。不知其仁；焉用佞？」

            
          


          

            
English · James Legge (1861/1893)

            
1.1The Master said of Kung-ye Ch'ang that he might be wived; although he was put in bonds, he had not been guilty of any crime. Accordingly, he gave him his own daughter to wife.
1.2Of Nan Yung he said that if the country were well governed he would not be out of office, and if it were ill-governed, he would escape punishment and disgrace. He gave him the daughter of his own elder brother to wife.
2The Master said of Tsze-chien, 'Of superior virtue indeed is such a man! If there were not virtuous men in Lu, how could this man have acquired this character?'
3Tsze-kung asked, 'What do you say of me, Ts'ze? The Master said, 'You are a utensil.' 'What utensil?' 'A gemmed sacrificial utensil.'
4.1Some one said, 'Yung is truly virtuous, but he is not ready with his tongue.'
4.2The Master said, 'What is the good of being ready with the tongue? They who encounter men with smartnesses of speech for the most part procure themselves hatred. I know not whether he be truly virtuous, but why should he show readiness of the tongue?'

5The Master was wishing Ch'i-tiao K'ai to enter on official employment. He replied, 'I am not yet able to rest in the assurance of THIS.' The Master was pleased.
6The Master said, 'My doctrines make no way. I will get upon a raft, and float about on the sea. He that will accompany me will be Yu, I dare say.' Tsze-lu hearing this was glad, upon which the Master said, 'Yu is fonder of daring than I am. He does not exercise his judgment upon matters.'
7.1Mang Wu asked about Tsze-lu, whether he was perfectly virtuous. The Master said, 'I do not know.'
7.2He asked again, when the Master replied, 'In a kingdom of a thousand chariots, Yu might be employed to manage the military levies, but I do not know whether he be perfectly virtuous.'

7.3'And what do you say of Ch'iu?' The Master replied, 'In a city of a thousand families, or a clan of a hundred chariots, Ch'iu might be employed as governor, but I do not know whether he is perfectly virtuous.'
7.4'What do you say of Ch'ih?' The Master replied, 'With his sash girt and standing in a court, Ch'ih might be employed to converse with the visitors and guests, but I do not know whether he is perfectly virtuous.'
8.1The Master said to Tsze-kung, 'Which do you consider superior, yourself or Hui?'
8.2Tsze-kung replied, 'How dare I compare myself with Hui? Hui hears one point and knows all about a subject; I hear one point, and know a second.'
8.3The Master said, 'You are not equal to him. I grant you, you are not equal to him.'

9.1Tsai Yu being asleep during the daytime, the Master said, 'Rotten wood cannot be carved; a wall of dirty earth will not receive the trowel. This Yu!-- what is the use of my reproving him?'
9.2The Master said, 'At first, my way with men was to hear their words, and give them credit for their conduct. Now my way is to hear their words, and look at their conduct. It is from Yu that I have learned to make this change.'
10The Master said, 'I have not seen a firm and unbending man.' Some one replied, 'There is Shan Ch'ang.' 'Ch'ang,' said the Master, 'is under the influence of his passions; how can he be pronounced firm and unbending?'
11Tsze-kung said, 'What I do not wish men to do to me, I also wish not to do to men.' The Master said, 'Ts'ze, you have not attained to that.'

12子貢曰：「夫子之文章，可得而聞也；夫子之言性與天道，不可得而聞也。」
13子路有聞，未之能行，唯恐有聞。

English · James Legge (1861/1893)

            
12Tsze-kung said, 'The Master's personal displays of his principles and ordinary descriptions of them may be heard. His discourses about man's nature, and the way of Heaven, cannot be heard.'
13When Tsze-lu heard anything, if he had not yet succeeded in carrying it into practice, he was only afraid lest he should hear something else.
14Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'On what ground did Kung-wan get that title of Wan?' The Master said, 'He was of an active nature and yet fond of learning, and he was not ashamed to ask and learn of his inferiors!-- On these grounds he has been styled Wan.'
15The Master said of Tsze-ch'an that he had four of the characteristics of a superior man:-- in his conduct of himself, he was humble; in serving his superiors, he was respectful; in nourishing the people, he was kind; in ordering the people, he was just.'

16The Master said, 'Yen P'ing knew well how to maintain friendly intercourse. The acquaintance might be long, but he showed the same respect as at first.'
17The Master said, 'Tsang Wan kept a large tortoise in a house, on the capitals of the pillars of which he had hills made, and with representations of duckweed on the small pillars above the beams supporting the rafters.-- Of what sort was his wisdom?'
18.1Tsze-chang asked, saying, 'The minister Tsze- wan thrice took office, and manifested no joy in his countenance. Thrice he retired from office, and manifested no displeasure. He made it a point to inform the new minister of the way in which he had conducted the government;-- what do you say of him?' The Master replied. 'He was loyal.' 'Was he perfectly virtuous?' 'I do not know. How can he be pronounced perfectly virtuous?'
18.2Tsze-chang proceeded, 'When the officer Ch'ui killed the prince of Ch'i, Ch'an Wan, though he was the owner of forty horses, abandoned them and left the country. Coming to another State, he said, "They are here like our great officer, Ch'ui," and left it. He came to a second State, and with the same observation left it also;-- what do you say of him?' The Master replied, 'He was pure.' 'Was he perfectly virtuous?' 'I do not know. How can he be pronounced perfectly virtuous?'

21When the Master was in Ch'an, he said, 'Let me return! Let me return! The little children of my school are ambitious and too hasty. They are accomplished and complete so far, but they do not know how to restrict and shape themselves.'

25.1Yen Yuan and Chi Lu being by his side, the Master said to them, 'Come, let each of you tell his wishes.'
