Introduction

        
The later (first-millennium) version of the opening of the story: the labour of the gods, their rebellion and the creation of humankind.
Atrahasis ('Exceedingly Wise') is the Babylonian story of humanity from its creation to the Flood. The lesser gods rebel against their labour; humankind is created from clay and the flesh and blood of a slain god to do the work instead; when people multiply and their noise disturbs Enlil, he sends plague, drought and famine, and finally the Flood, which Atrahasis survives in a boat on the advice of Enki (Ea). The oldest near-complete copy was written at Sippar by the scribe Ipiq-Aya in the 17th century BCE. This page follows the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition.
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/1/SB/I) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so (UM.55-21-29). The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is B. R. Foster's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the text; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 43 of the 123 lines of the eBL edition (35%), from 4 tablets in the British Museum and Penn Museum: K.10082 (Nineveh), K.6235 (Nineveh), UM.55-21-29 (Nippur), K.6831 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 80 of the 123 lines of the eBL edition are not shown, because no photographed copy of the work used here carries them: 1-40 (not in the eBL Fragmentarium (no photograph there): IM.124646); 48-58 (not in the eBL Fragmentarium (no photograph there): IM.124646); 76 (not in the eBL Fragmentarium (no photograph there): IM.124646); 81-97 (not in the eBL Fragmentarium (no photograph there): IM.124646); 112-120 (not in the eBL Fragmentarium (no photograph there): IM.124646; parallel text: IM.124649). A line is shown only on a photographed tablet that carries it as text. Commentaries and other texts that quote a line (letters, rituals, god lists) are not used as witnesses, because they carry it with explanations or inside another text; tablets without a photograph in eBL are not used, because the line could not be shown on its tablet.

            §75-80 · UM.55-21-29, reverse, lines 1 to 5' of the tablet = SB I 75-80
            

              
              
              
            

          


          
          

            
Manuscript

            
            
            
            
Where on the image
Penn Museum UM.55-21-29: reverse, the tablet's own lines 1 to 5'. eBL's corpus edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/1/SB/I) attributes SB I 75-80 to this manuscript at these column and line numbers. Photograph checked (eBL photograph of the Penn Museum): obverse, reverse and edges of a fragment about 5 x 4.5 cm by the scale bar, ink 55-21-29 and a Nippur field number on the right edge. In eBL's standard layout the obverse is the upper central view (about two lines at the top right, a ruling, then about five or six lines) and the reverse the lower central view: a block of about five lines at its upper left keeps the line beginnings and loses the line ends to a diagonal break, as eBL's reverse lines 1 and 2'-5' do (75, 77-80; line 76 omitted by the scribe), and a few further lines at its lower right are broken at the left. The Penn catalogue counts 11 lines on each face. eBL classes UM.55-21-29 as a school tablet (a scribal exercise that copies an excerpt of the text), not a library copy of the whole work. It carries these lines as text (eBL's transliteration has no commentary or quotation markup), and it is used because no photographed library copy preserves these lines as well. On this tablet lines 75, 77-80 are partly broken (square brackets and x in the Akkadian). The English of these lines translates eBL's composite text, so it includes words that are not preserved on this tablet but are restored from other manuscripts or by the editors. Square brackets in the English mark eBL's restorations in its composite text; words that survive on other tablets carry no brackets even where this tablet is broken. eBL line(s) 76 fall within this run but are not on this tablet (they are listed among the lines not shown).
