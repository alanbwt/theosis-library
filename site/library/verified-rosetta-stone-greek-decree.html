Introduction

        
The Rosetta Stone (British Museum EA 24) is a granodiorite stela found in 1799 at Rashid (Rosetta) in the western Nile Delta. It carries one decree in three scripts: Egyptian hieroglyphs at the top (14 surviving lines), Egyptian Demotic in the middle (32 lines) and Greek at the bottom (54 lines). The decree was passed by a synod of Egyptian priests at Memphis on 27 March 196 BCE (4 Xandikos, 18 Mecheir, year 9) to honour the young king Ptolemy V Epiphanes after his coronation. Because the Greek could be read, the stone gave the key to the decipherment of hieroglyphs (Thomas Young, Jean-Francois Champollion, 1814-1822).



The Greek text lists the king's benefactions to the temples (tax remissions, release of prisoners, gifts to the sacred bulls Apis and Mnevis, the siege of rebel Lykopolis) and then the priests' resolutions: statues and golden shrines of the king in every temple, monthly and yearly festivals, a new priestly title, and the order to set up the decree on stelae of hard stone 'in holy, native and Greek letters', which is the stone itself.



This entry gives the complete Greek text, all 54 lines, in nine sections of six lines. The Greek is the line-by-line transcript printed by E. A. Wallis Budge in The Decrees of Memphis and Canopus (1904), with the stone's line numbers; letters in square brackets are restorations where the right-hand and lower edges of the stone are broken. The English is Budge's own rendering of the Greek in the same book, numbered by the same lines; words in square brackets there are Budge's additions for sense. Each section is shown on the British Museum's 1922 photograph of the stone, cut and straightened to the six lines of that section, together with the whole Greek register. The hieroglyphic and Demotic versions are not given here because no openly licensed digital text of them was found.
