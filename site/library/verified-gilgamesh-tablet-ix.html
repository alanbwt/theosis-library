Introduction

        
Afraid of death, Gilgamesh roams the wild in search of Uta-napishti, the survivor of the Flood. He passes the scorpion-people guarding the mountains of sunrise and walks the dark path of the sun to a garden of jewelled trees.
The Standard Babylonian version of the epic, traditionally ascribed to the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni, was written on twelve tablets. Most surviving copies come from the libraries of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and from Babylon and other Babylonian cities (7th-2nd century BCE). This page gives Tablet IX in the line numbering of the electronic Babylonian Library edition by A. R. George (2022 and later revisions).
How this page is built. Each section is a run of consecutive lines as written on one photographed clay tablet. For every line the eBL edition (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/IX) lists the manuscripts that carry it, with each tablet's own column and line numbers; the tablet chosen for each run is the photographed copy of the work with the most signs preserved there. Commentaries and texts that only quote a line are never chosen. School and excerpt tablets are used only for lines that no photographed library copy preserves as well, and only where they carry the line as text; their sections say so. The original text is eBL's transliteration of that tablet's signs (not a modern composite text): square brackets mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign, ? an uncertain reading, and words in {braces} are determinatives; the notes give a key to every sign used on this page, and eBL's layout codes are written out in words (for example '(line break)' or '(blank space)'). Superscript numbers are the line numbers of the eBL edition, used in both the Akkadian and the English. The English is A. R. George's translation in the eBL edition (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), one line of English per line of the poem; italics mark uncertain renderings, as in the source. Where the tablet is broken, the English still translates the whole line of eBL's composite text; each section's note names the lines that are broken on its tablet.
Finding a line on the photograph. Each section names the tablet, the face and column, and the tablet's own line numbers (a prime, as in 5', counts from the first preserved line of a broken column). On a tablet with six columns the obverse carries columns i-iii from left to right; the reverse, turned over from bottom to top, carries columns iv-vi from right to left. The photographs often show the obverse above the reverse with the edges around them; a fragment preserves only part of this layout.
This page shows 135 of the 137 lines of the eBL edition (99%), from 2 tablets in the British Museum: K.2360 (Nineveh), K.18183 (Nineveh).
Lines not shown. 2 of the 137 lines of the eBL edition are not shown, because no photographed copy of the work used here carries them: 146-147 (no manuscript preserves it). A line is shown only on a photographed tablet that carries it as text. Commentaries and other texts that quote a line (letters, rituals, god lists) are not used as witnesses, because they carry it with explanations or inside another text; tablets without a photograph in eBL are not used, because the line could not be shown on its tablet.

English · A. R. George (eBL edition)

                    
37The name of the mountain was Māšu.

38To Māšu’s twin mountains he came,

39which daily guard the rising [of the sun,] –

40their tops [abut] the fabric of the heavens,

41their bases reach down to Hades –

42there were scorpion-men guarding its gate,

43whose terror was dread and glance was death,

44whose radiance was terrifying, enveloping the uplands –

45at both sunrise and sunset they guard the sun –

46Gilgameš saw them and covered his face with fear and dread;

47he collected his wits and drew near their presence.

48The scorpion-man called to his female:

49‘He who has come to us, flesh of the gods is his body.’

50The scorpion-man’s female answered him:

51‘Two-thirds of him are god but a third of him is human.’

52The male scorpion-man called out,

53saying a word [to King Gilgameš,] flesh of the gods:

54'[How did you come here,] a far road?

55[how did you get] here, into my presence?

56[How did you ford the many rivers], whose crossing is perilous?

57[…] let me learn of your […]

58[… where] your [face] is set,

59[… let me] learn [of your journey.]'
