Introduction

        
Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh (Standard Babylonian version, attributed to Sin-leqi-unninni) as it survives on one physical object: British Museum tablet K.2252 (K.2252 + K.2602 + K.3321 + K.4486 + Sm.1881 (joined)), excavated at Nineveh from the library of Ashurbanipal (7th c. BCE). It is manuscript C in A. R. George's 2003 edition. The photograph is the British Museum's, showing the inscribed face(s) and edges; column(s) i, ii, iii, iv, v, vi are preserved (268 lines).
Three criteria are met for every section:
  1. Manuscript scan: the British Museum photograph of K.2252 itself (served by the Electronic Babylonian Library, LMU Munich). Each section names the column and face (obverse = columns i-iii, reverse = columns iv-vi) and the tablet's own line numbers, so the reader can find the lines on the photograph.
  2. Original text: the eBL manuscript-level ATF transliteration of the signs on this tablet (not the composite text). Brackets [ ] mark broken signs, x an illegible sign, # a damaged sign. Superscript numbers are the modern line numbers of George 2003 / eBL.
  3. Public-domain English: R. Campbell Thompson, The Epic of Gilgamish (London, 1928), verbatim, with his marginal line numbers as T n.
On line numbers. Thompson's 1928 numbering is not George's. For this tablet the correspondence was established one anchor per Thompson marginal number by reading his verse against the Akkadian; sections are cut only at those anchors, so each section's English is the block of Thompson's lines that renders exactly these Akkadian lines. Where the tablet's text begins or ends inside a Thompson block, the whole block is given and the section says so. Thompson's readings reflect the state of knowledge in 1928: modern editions (George 2003; the eBL edition, updated continuously) supersede him and are not public domain, so they are not reproduced here beyond the transliteration of this tablet's signs.

The City Shurippak —

(O ’tis) a city thou knowest!—is set [on the marge] of Euphrates,

Old is this city, with gods in its midst. (Now), the great gods a deluge

Purposed to bring: . . . . . . there was Anu, their sire; their adviser

Warrior Enlil; Ninurta , their herald; their leader(?) Ennugi;

Nin-igi-azag—’tis Ea—, (albeit) conspirator with them,


T 20Unto a reed-hut their counsel betray’d he: "O Reed-hut, O Reed-hut!

Thou of Shurippak, thou scion of Ubara-Tutu, a dwelling


T 25Pull down, (and) fashion a vessel (therewith); abandon possessions,

Life do thou seek, (and) thy hoard disregard, and save life; every creature

Make to embark in the vessel. The vessel, which thou art to fashion,


T 30Apt be its measure; its beam and its length be in due correspondence,

(Then) [on] the deep do thou launch it." And I—sooth, I apprehending,

(This wise) to Ea, my lord, did I speak: '[See], Lord, what thou sayest


T 35Thus, do I honour, I'll do—(but) to city, to people, and elders

Am I, forsooth, to explain?' (Then) Ea made answer in speaking,

Saying to me—me, his henchman!—'Thou mortal, shalt speak to them this wise:

"’Tis me alone (?) whom Enlil so hateth that I in your city


T 40No (more) may dwell, nor turn my face unto the land which is Enlil's.

[I will go] down to the Deep, (there) dwelling with Ea, my [liege] lord,

(Wherefore) [on] you will he shower down plenty, yea, fowl [in great number(?)],


T 45Booty of fish . . . . [and big] the harvest.

. . . . . . . . causing a plentiful rainfall (?) to come down upon you."'

[(Then) when something] of morning had dawn’d . . . .


(Five lines mutilated).

(Then) on the fifth day (after) I laid out the  shape (of my vessel),

Ten gar each was the height of her sides, in accord with her planning(?),

Ten gar to match was the size of her deck (?), and the shape of the forepart (?)


T 60Did I lay down, (and) the same did I fashion; (aye), six times cross-pinn’d her,

Sevenfold did I divide her . . . ., divided her inwards

Ninefold: hammer’d the caulking within her, (and) found me a quant-pole,


T 65(All) that was needful I added; the hull with six  shar of bitumen

Smear’d I, (and) three shar of pitch [did I smear] on the inside; some people,

Bearing a vessel of grease, three shar of it brought (me); (and) one shar

(Out of this) grease did I leave, which the tackling (?) consumed; (and) the boatman


T 70Two shar of grease stow’d away; (yea), beeves for the . . . I slaughter’d,

Each day lambs did I slay: mead, beer, oil, wine, too, the workmen

[Drank] as though they were water , and made a great feast like the New Year,
