Introduction

        
Chapter 67 of the Daodejing (Laozi), shown on the two oldest nearly complete copies of the work: the silk manuscripts Laozi A and Laozi B, found in 1973 in Tomb 3 at Mawangdui, Changsha (Hunan), a tomb sealed in 168 BCE. Laozi A is written in a script between seal and clerical forms and does not avoid the personal name of the Han founder Liu Bang (邦); it is commonly dated before 195 BCE. Laozi B is in clerical script, avoids 邦, and is commonly dated 194-180 BCE. Both put the De part (chapters 38-81) before the Dao part (chapters 1-37), and a few chapters stand in a different order from the received text.
Each section gives the silk transcription column by column, with the Jicheng column number before each column (for Laozi B, 上 and 下 are the upper and lower halves of a column, photographed on facing plates), and links to the plate that carries those columns. □ marks a lost character; ∠, ‧ and ﹦ are marks written on the silk (﹦ repeats the character before it). Below the transcription is the received text of the Wang Bi recension, which is the text James Legge translated in 1891; the silk wording differs from it, most visibly in 恆 for received 常 (the received text avoids the name of Emperor Wen, Liu Heng), in the frequent final particle 也, and in loan characters such as 胃 for 謂.

Manuscript

            
            
            
            
Where on the image
Laozi A (甲本), columns 67 to 70, on plate p. 98 of Changsha Mawangdui Han mu jianbo jicheng, vol. 1 (column numbers are printed under each plate; the columns run from right to left). Checked on the image: column 68 reads 夫唯□故不宵∠若宵∠細久矣我恆有三葆之. The opening characters of this chapter are lost or not readable on this plate image, so these are the first characters that were confirmed. In this manuscript the chapter follows chapter 81, not chapter 66. □ marks a character lost on the silk (25 in this section). Transcription and column numbers: Wikisource 老子 (帛書甲本), which follows the Jicheng column numbering. The English is Legge's translation of the received text, shown below the silk transcription; the silk wording differs from it in many places, so compare the two Chinese texts for the variants.

          

          

          

            
Classical Chinese · Mawangdui silk transcription

                    
67□□□□□□□□
68夫唯□故不宵∠若宵∠細久矣我恆有三葆之∠一曰茲二曰檢□□□□□□□□□□□□□□
69故能廣不敢為天下先故能為成事長∠今舍亓茲且勇∠舍亓後且先則必死矣夫茲□□
70則勝以守則固天將建之女以茲垣之

            
            

              Compare: Received text (Wang Bi recension), the text Legge translated
              
天下皆謂我道大，似不肖。夫唯大，故似不肖。若肖，久矣其細也夫！我有三寶，持而保之。一曰慈，二曰儉，三曰不敢為天下先。慈故能勇，儉故能廣，不敢為天下先，故能成器長。今舍慈且勇，舍儉且廣，舍後且先，死矣！夫慈以戰則勝，以守則固。天將救之，以慈衛之。

            

            
          


          

            
English · James Legge (1891)

            
1All the world says that, while my Tao is great, it yet appears to be inferior (to other systems of teaching). Now it is just its greatness that makes it seem to be inferior. If it were like any other (system), for long would its smallness have been known!
2But I have three precious things which I prize and hold fast. The first is gentleness; the second is economy; and the third is shrinking from taking precedence of others.
3With that gentleness I can be bold; with that economy I can be liberal; shrinking from taking precedence of others, I can become a vessel of the highest honour. Now-a-days they give up gentleness and are all for being bold; economy, and are all for being liberal; the hindmost place, and seek only to be foremost;--(of all which the end is) death.
4Gentleness is sure to be victorious even in battle, and firmly to maintain its ground. Heaven will save its possessor, by his (very) gentleness protecting him.
