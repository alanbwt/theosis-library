Introduction

        
The Poimandres is the first treatise of the Corpus Hermeticum, a collection of Greek dialogues and sermons ascribed to Hermes Trismegistus, written in Roman Egypt probably in the 2nd or 3rd century AD. The narrator falls into a trance and meets Poimandres (in Mead's translation 'Man-Shepherd, Mind of all-masterhood'), who shows him in a vision how the world came to be: light and a holy Word (Logos) come out of the divine Mind, a second creator-Mind makes the seven planetary governors, and the archetypal Man, made in the image of the Father, descends through the spheres, falls in love with Nature and becomes mortal in body but immortal in essence (sections 12-15). Poimandres then teaches the way back: at death the soul gives up its passions to each of the seven zones, enters the eighth nature, hymns the Father with the Powers, which is 'the good end for those who have gained Gnosis' (26); the Greek word for the end is theothenai, 'to be made god' (Mead: 'to be made one with God'). The narrator begins to preach to mankind and closes with a hymn, 'Holy art Thou, O God, the universals' Father' (31-32).



The pages shown are from Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 71.33, a paper manuscript of the 14th century (manuscript A of the editions), the oldest copy of the Corpus Hermeticum with page images online; it later belonged to Marsilio Ficino, whose Latin translation of 1463 made the Hermetica famous in the Renaissance. The Poimandres fills fols. 123r-125v. Each section below is one of the 32 standard sections, linked to the page or pages that carry its words, identified by reading the first and last lines of each page on the image. The Greek is Gustav Parthey's edition of 1854, which differs from this manuscript and from later editions in places (in section 5 the manuscript has blank spaces where its exemplar was damaged). The English is G. R. S. Mead's translation of 1906, made from Reitzenstein's text of 1904, so a few phrases do not match Parthey's Greek word for word.

English · G. R. S. Mead (1906)

                    
It chanced once on a time my mind was meditating on the things that are, my thought was raised to a great height, the senses of my body being held back — just as men are who are weighed down with sleep after a fill of food, or from fatigue of body.
Methought a Being more than vast, in size beyond all bounds, called out my name and saith: What wouldst thou hear and see, and what hast thou in mind to learn and know?

English · G. R. S. Mead (1906)

                    
And some of them with jests upon their lips departed [from me], abandoning themselves unto the Way of Death; others entreated to be taught, casting themselves before my feet.

And when even was come and all sun's beams began to set, I bade them all give thanks to God. And when they had brought to an end the giving of their thanks, each man returned to his own resting place.
