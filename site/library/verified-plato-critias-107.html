Introduction

        
Stephanus page 107 of Plato's Critias (107a-107e): Critias asks for indulgence: talk about mortals is judged more strictly than talk about gods.



The Critias is Plato's full account of Atlantis and of the Athens that fought it: the land and city of ancient Athens (109b-112e), then the island of Atlantis, Poseidon's kingdom beyond the Pillars of Heracles, its royal city, plain, army and laws (113b-120d), and the moral decline of its kings (120e-121c). Plato left the dialogue unfinished; it stops in mid-sentence as Zeus begins to speak, and the manuscript shown here stops at the same words.



The manuscript is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, grec 1807, known to editors as Plato A. It was copied in Constantinople around 850-875 by the scribe of the so-called philosophical collection, and is the oldest surviving medieval manuscript of the Timaeus and Critias (tetralogies VIII-IX: Clitophon, Republic, Timaeus, Critias, Minos, Laws, Epinomis, Letters). Each page has two columns of 44 lines. The Timaeus fills fol. 114r-144v and the Critias fol. 145r-151r.



On the manuscript this page of text runs over fol. 145r col. a to fol. 145v col. a. Each section below is linked to the folio side(s) that carry it. The mapping comes from reading the first words of every column on the images and finding them in Burnet's text: fol. 145r col. a begins ‹ὡς ἅσμενος, ὦ Σώκρατες, οἷον ἐκ› (Crit. 106a); fol. 145r col. b begins ‹θνητῶν πρὸς ἡμᾶς. ἡ γὰρ ἀπειρία› (Crit. 107b); fol. 145v col. a begins ‹-τὰ καὶ ἀνθρώπινα ἀκριβῶς ἐξετάζομεν. ἐκ› (Crit. 107d).



The Greek is John Burnet's critical text (Oxford, 1902), not a transcription of this manuscript; the scribe's spelling, accents and occasional readings differ from it. The English is R. G. Bury's Loeb translation (1929).

Greek · Burnet

ΚΡΙ. καίτοι σχεδὸν μὲν οἶδα παραίτησιν εὖ μάλα φιλότιμον καὶ τοῦ δέοντος ἀγροικοτέραν μέλλων παραιτεῖσθαι, ῥητέον δὲ ὅμως. ὡς μὲν γὰρ οὐκ εὖ τὰ παρὰ σοῦ λεχθέντα εἴρηται, τίς ἂν ἐπιχειρήσειεν ἔμφρων λέγειν; ὅτι δὲ τὰ ῥηθησόμενα πλείονος συγγνώμης δεῖται χαλεπώτερα ὄντα, τοῦτο πειρατέον πῃ διδάξαι. περὶ θεῶν γάρ, ὦ Τίμαιε, λέγοντά τι πρὸς ἀνθρώπους δοκεῖν ἱκανῶς

            
          


          

            
English · R. G. Bury (1929)

            
in respect of the discourse I am about to deliver.


Crit. I am sufficiently aware that the request I am about to make is decidedly presumptuous and less civil than is proper, but none the less it must be uttered. For as regards the exposition you gave, what man in his senses would attempt to deny its excellence? But what I must somehow endeavor to show is that the discourse now to be delivered calls for greater indulgence because of its greater difficulty. For it is easier, Timaeus, to appear to speak satisfactorily to men about the gods,
