Introduction

        
P46 is a papyrus codex of the letters of Paul copied in Egypt around 200, the oldest surviving collection of Paul's letters. 86 of its 104 leaves survive: 56 in the Chester Beatty Library, Dublin (Biblical Papyrus II, published by Frederic Kenyon in 1934-1937) and 30 in the University of Michigan Library, Ann Arbor (P.Mich. inv. 6238, published by Henry Sanders in 1935). The letters run in the order Romans, Hebrews, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Ephesians, Galatians, Philippians, Colossians and 1 Thessalonians; the first seven leaves (Romans 1:1-5:17) and the end of the book are lost. Hebrews standing after Romans, and the doxology of Romans (16:25-27) standing after 15:33, are among its best-known features.



Each section below is one page side: the holding library's photograph, the NTVMR diplomatic transcription line by line, and the King James Version of every verse that has text on the page. The lower lines of many leaves are lost; the transcribers' restorations are in square brackets.



This entry covers fols. 48r-49r (1 Corinthians 9:4-10:1).

English · King James Version (1611)

                    
9:4Have we not power to eat and to drink? 5Have we not power to lead about a sister, a wife, as well as other apostles, and as the brethren of the Lord, and Cephas? 6Or I only and Barnabas, have not we power to forbear working? 7Who goeth a warfare any time at his own charges? who planteth a vineyard, and eateth not of the fruit thereof? or who feedeth a flock, and eateth not of the milk of the flock? 8Say I these things as a man? or saith not the law the same also? 9For it is written in the law of Moses, Thou shalt not muzzle the mouth of the ox that treadeth out the corn. Doth God take care for oxen? 10Or saith he it altogether for our sakes? For our sakes, no doubt, this is written: that he that ploweth should plow in hope; and that he that thresheth in hope should be partaker of his hope. 11If we have sown unto you spiritual things, is it a great thing if we shall reap your carnal things? 12If others be partakers of this power over you, are not we rather? Nevertheless we have not used this power; but suffer all things, lest we should hinder the gospel of Christ.

English · King James Version (1611)

                    
9:20And unto the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews; to them that are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain them that are under the law; 21To them that are without law, as without law, (being not without law to God, but under the law to Christ,) that I might gain them that are without law. 22To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some. 23And this I do for the gospel’s sake, that I might be partaker thereof with you. 24Know ye not that they which run in a race run all, but one receiveth the prize? So run, that ye may obtain. 25And every man that striveth for the mastery is temperate in all things. Now they do it to obtain a corruptible crown; but we an incorruptible. 26I therefore so run, not as uncertainly; so fight I, not as one that beateth the air: 27But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway. 10:1Moreover, brethren, I would not that ye should be ignorant, how that all our fathers were under the cloud, and all passed through the sea;

          

        

        
      


    

    

    

      
      

        
Notes

        
    
          
          
  1. 
            Greek: the NTVMR diplomatic transcription, line by line as on the papyrus. Square brackets enclose letters that are lost or illegible and were supplied by the transcribers; a dot under a letter marks it as uncertain; [...] marks a gap. Nomina sacra and numerals keep the scribe's overline. Spelling is the scribe's (no accents or breathings). Words broken at a line end stay broken as on the papyrus. Corrections are not shown: the text is the first hand.
          
    2. 
          
          
  2. 
            English: the King James Version (1611), whole verses, for every verse that has text on the page. A verse that begins on one page and ends on the next is given in full on both. The KJV translates a later form of the Greek text, so its wording does not always match the papyrus; material differences (such as verses the papyrus does not have) are noted in the section.
