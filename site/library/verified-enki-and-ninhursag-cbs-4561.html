Introduction

        
Enki and Ninhursaĝa is a Sumerian myth set in Dilmun, a land that is pure and clean, where no lion kills and no one is sick or old. At the request of the goddess Ninsikila, Enki brings fresh water to Dilmun and makes it a prosperous trading city. He then lies with Ninhursaĝa (Nintur) and with their daughter and granddaughters in turn. Ninhursaĝa makes eight plants grow from Enki's seed; Enki eats them, and she curses him. He falls ill, and only when she returns does he recover: for each part of his body that hurts she brings a god into being, and the poem ends by assigning their roles. Because of its garden setting, the forbidden plants and the goddess born for Enki's rib (Nin-ti, 'lady of the rib' or 'lady who gives life'), it has often been compared with Genesis 2-3.
The main manuscript, shown here, is CBS 4561 in the Penn Museum, Philadelphia: a six-column tablet from Nippur, Old Babylonian period (about 1800-1600 BCE). Stephen Langdon published it in 1915 as 'The Sumerian Epic of Paradise, the Flood and the Fall of Man'; his readings were soon corrected, and the standard modern text is ETCSL's, used here. Two other manuscripts add lines: a tablet from Ur (UET 6/1 1) and one in the Louvre (AO 6724).
Each section below is one paragraph of the ETCSL translation, with the face of CBS 4561 that carries it and the column marked in the transliteration.
Gaps. Two passages are not on CBS 4561 and are not shown: lines 49A-49V, Enki's blessing listing the lands that will send goods to Dilmun (Tukriš, Meluḫḫa, Marḫaši, Magan, the Sealand, Zalamgar, Elam, Ur), known from the Ur tablet, and lines 126A-126CC, a further generation of Enki's daughters, known from the Louvre tablet. Neither tablet has a usable published photograph at CDLI. The breaks at the foot of obverse column i and the head of each reverse column are noted in the sections concerned.
