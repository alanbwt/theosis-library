Introduction

        
Philo of Byblos, a Phoenician grammarian writing in Greek around 100 AD, claimed to have translated the Phoenician History of Sanchuniathon of Berytus, a priestly writer said to have lived before the Trojan War, who drew on the city records and the temple inscriptions of Phoenicia and on the writings of Taautus (Thoth). Philo's work survives only in the long quotations that Eusebius of Caesarea copied into book 1 of his Preparation for the Gospel (c. 313 AD), taken partly through the philosopher Porphyry. It is the fullest account of Phoenician myth in Greek, and since the discovery of the Ugaritic tablets (14th-13th c. BCE) several of its gods and stories, such as El, Dagon, the succession of divine kings and the castration of the sky god, are known to go back to Bronze Age Canaan.



The manuscript shown is Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, grec 451, written in 914 AD by the scribe Baanes for Arethas, archbishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia; it is the oldest manuscript of books 1-5 of the Preparation. The passage runs from fol. 204v to fol. 213v. Each section is one of Dindorf's numbered sections and points at the page(s) that carry it; the page breaks were read on the images and are recorded in each note.



This entry covers sections 1.10.42 to 1.10.55, on fols. 211v to 213v.

English · E. H. Gifford (1903)

                    
10.44And soon after he says: 'It was a custom of the ancients in great crises of danger for the rulers of a city or nation, in order to avert the common ruin, to give up the most beloved of their children for sacrifice as a ransom to the avenging daemons; and those who were thus given up were sacrificed with mystic rites. Kronos then, whom the Phoenicians call Elus, who was king of the country and subsequently, after his decease, was deified as the star Saturn, had by a nymph of the country named Anobret an only begotten son, whom they on this account called Iedud, the only begotten being still so called among the Phoenicians; and when very great dangers from war had beset the country, he arrayed his son in royal apparel, and prepared an altar, and sacrificed him.'

10.46'The nature then of the dragon and of serpents Tauthus himself regarded as divine, and so again after him did the Phoenicians and Egyptians: for this animal was declared by him to be of all reptiles most full of breath, and fiery. In consequence of which it also exerts an unsurpassable swiftness by means of its breath, without feet and hands or any other of the external members by which the other animals make their movements. It also exhibits forms of various shapes, and in its progress makes spiral leaps as swift as it chooses.

10.51'Moreover the Egyptians, describing the world from the same idea, engrave the circumference of a circle, of the colour of the sky and of fire, and a hawk-shaped serpent stretched across the middle of it, and the whole shape is like our Theta (θ), representing the circle as the world, and signifying by the serpent which connects it in the middle the good daemon.

English · E. H. Gifford (1903)

                    
10.52'Zoroaster also the Magian, in the Sacred Collection of Persian Records, says in express words: "And god has the head of a hawk. He is the first, incorruptible, eternal, uncreate, without parts, most unlike (all else), the controller of all good, who cannot be bribed, the best of all the good, the wisest of all wise; and he is also a father of good laws and justice, self-taught, natural, and perfect, and wise, and the sole author of the sacred power of nature. 'The same also is said of him by Ostanes in the book entitled Octateuch.'

So much concerning serpents.

10.55It must be manifest that these are not fables and poets' fictions containing some theory concealed in hidden meanings, but true testimonies, as they would themselves say, of wise and ancient theologians, containing things of earlier date than all poets and historians, and deriving the credibility of their statements from the names and history of the gods still prevailing in the cities and villages of Phoenicia, and from the mysteries celebrated among each people: so that it is no longer necessary to search out violent physical explanations of these things, since the evidence which the facts bring with them of themselves is quite clear. Such then is the theology of the Phoenicians: but it is now time to pass on and examine carefully the case of the Egyptians.
